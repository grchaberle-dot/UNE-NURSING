## What the Lymphatic System Does

A network of tiny vessels that collect fluid and waste products from body tissues. A vital part of both the **immune** and **circulatory** systems.

**Functions:**
- Transports immune cells to where they are needed
- Maintains fluid balance throughout the body
- Filters potentially harmful material

### Components

- **Lymph vessels:** collect and transport lymph
- **Lymph nodes:** small rounded glands (1 mm to 25 mm) that trap and remove harmful substances such as bacteria or cancer cells. Usually 15 to 30 in each armpit. Found in armpit, groin, stomach, chest and neck
- **Spleen:** filters blood, stores immune cells
- **Thymus:** where T cells mature
- **MALT** (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue): immune tissue in mucosal surfaces
- **Bone marrow:** produces lymphocytes and other blood cells
- **Lymphocytes:** specialised immune cells (B cells and T cells)

---

## How Lymph Forms

1. Some fluid is forced out of the bloodstream at the **arterial end** of blood capillaries
2. Most is reabsorbed at the **venous end**
3. The remaining fluid combines with **interstitial fluid** surrounding tissue cells
4. This leftover fluid returns to the bloodstream through the lymphatic system
5. Once interstitial fluid enters a lymphatic vessel, it is called **lymph**

**Interstitial fluid may contain:** proteins, cell debris, pathogens, mutated cells, fatty acids.

Lymph is usually clear. When it receives fluid containing fatty acids from the intestines it becomes cloudy. This cloudy lymph is called **chyle**.

---

## Lymphatic Vessels

Found throughout the body **except in bone, bone marrow and teeth**.

### Structure of lymphatic capillaries

Differ from blood capillaries because their endothelial cells are **not tightly joined**. These cells are anchored by **collagen fibres**.

- **Collagen fibres:** prevent the vessels collapsing when pressure from surrounding interstitial fluid becomes too great
- **Endothelial cell openings:** snap shut when pressure inside the lymphatic vessel becomes too high, preventing additional fluid entering

---

## Lymph Flow Pathway

Lymph flows in one direction, generally from the feet and other tissues toward the heart.

1. Interstitial fluid enters lymphatic vessels and becomes lymph
2. Lymph travels through collecting lymphatic vessels
3. Lymph passes through lymph nodes, where it is filtered and cleaned
4. Collecting vessels merge to form lymphatic trunks
5. The trunks drain into the right lymphatic duct or the thoracic duct
6. The ducts empty lymph into the right or left subclavian vein
7. Lymph becomes part of the bloodstream

---

## Lymphatic Trunks and Ducts

Main trunks: **lumbar, intestinal, bronchomediastinal, subclavian, jugular**.

### Right lymphatic duct

Collects from:
- Right bronchomediastinal trunk (drains right lung region)
- Right subclavian trunk (drains right upper limb)
- Right jugular trunk

**Drains:** the right upper region of the body only.

### Thoracic duct

Collects from:
- Left and right lumbar trunks
- Intestinal trunk
- Left bronchomediastinal trunk
- Left subclavian trunk
- Left jugular trunk

**Drains:** most of the rest of the body.

### Cisterna chyli

A wide lymphatic reservoir at the lower end of the thoracic duct region, around the level of the twelfth thoracic and second lumbar vertebrae.

> This asymmetry matters clinically for lymphoedema after breast surgery.

## Lymph Node Anatomy

Each node is enclosed in a fibrous capsule that divides it into compartments.

| Region | Contents |
|---|---|
| Cortex | Germinal centres where B cells grow and develop. Also circulating surveillance T cells and dendritic cells |
| Medullary cords | Contain B cells and T cells |
| Lymphatic sinuses | Extend through cortex and medulla. Contain **macrophages** that destroy foreign material |

## Lymphoid Tissue

Mainly composed of **reticular connective tissue**, which provides a structured framework for most lymphoid organs. The thymus is an exception.

Two main forms:
- **Diffuse lymphoid tissue:** found beneath epithelial membranes
- **Lymphoid nodules:** exist independently in locations such as the small intestine and appendix

---

## Primary vs Secondary Lymphoid Organs

### Primary (lymphocyte maturation)

- **Bone marrow:** B cells mature here
- **Thymus:** T cells move here to mature

### Secondary (pathogen encounter and immune response)

- Lymph nodes
- Spleen
- MALT
- Tonsils
- Peyer's patches
- Appendix

> B cells mature in **B**one marrow. T cells mature in the **T**hymus.

## The Adaptive Immune Response

When pathogens are **not** destroyed by the innate immune system, the adaptive immune response takes over. Highly specific, involves **lymphocytes**, takes place in the lymphatic system.

**Two paths:** the humoral response (antibodies) and the cell-mediated response (cytotoxic T cells).

### 1. The humoral response

Commonly activated to fight **bacterial infections or toxins**. Relies on **antibodies** to destroy foreign antigens.

Involves **clonal selection**: specific cells are selected and cloned to fight a specific target. Cells that are clonally selected then clonally select other immune cells, creating rapid expansion in lymphocyte numbers targeted to a specific pathogen.

> This rapid expansion is one reason an infected person feels fatigue.

### Antibodies

- Small **Y-shaped proteins** secreted by **plasma B cells**
- Destroy or deactivate the pathogen when they bind to it
- Highly specific to a particular antigen
- The level of antibodies in blood indicates the strength of immunity to that pathogen
- Four main functions when binding pathogens: neutralisation, opsonisation, complement activation, agglutination

### 2. The cell-mediated response

Commonly activated to fight **cancerous cells** or cells **infected by viruses**. Uses cells such as **cytotoxic T cells** to destroy other diseased cells.

Like the humoral response, involves a cascade of cells being activated and dividing. This large consumption of resources contributes to symptoms such as fatigue.

## Active and Passive Immunity

### Active immunity

The body responds to a pathogen using the adaptive immune response and **memory cells are formed**. Can last many years. Leads to an enhanced immune response to future infections (**immunological memory**).

Memory cells (memory B cells, memory T cells) form on first exposure (the primary response). In future infections there is a secondary response that is faster acting and enhanced.

- **Active natural:** immunity from catching the infection
- **Active artificial:** immunity from vaccination

### Passive immunity

A person is **given antibodies from an external source** rather than making them internally. Short lasting, does not activate the adaptive immune response, does not form memory cells.

- **Passive natural:** antibodies passed from mother to baby (breast milk, placenta)
- **Passive artificial:** antibodies given as medication (immunoglobulin injection)

### Summary table

| Type | How acquired | Duration | Memory cells? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Active natural | Catching the infection | Long lasting | Yes | Cold, chickenpox |
| Active artificial | Vaccination | Long lasting | Yes | COVID vaccine, flu shot |
| Passive natural | Mother to baby | Short | No | Breast milk antibodies |
| Passive artificial | Antibody injection | Short | No | Immunoglobulin therapy |

## Herd Immunity

Protection provided to **non-immunised individuals** living in a population of mostly immunised people. The spread of disease is unlikely in a highly immunised population, limiting exposure for those who are not immunised.

Requires a high percentage of the population to be immunised to be effective.
