## Amino Acids

### General structure

A central **alpha carbon** attached to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom and a variable side chain called the **R group**. The R group determines size, charge, polarity, hydrophobicity, hydrogen-bonding capacity and chemical reactivity.

Protein amino acids are predominantly the **L isomers**. L and D amino acids are enantiomers (non-superimposable mirror images). Biological systems are stereospecific, so proteins generally recognise L amino acids but not their D counterparts.

### Ionisation and pH

At approximately neutral pH, free amino acids exist as **zwitterions**. The amino group is protonated and positively charged, the carboxyl group deprotonated and negatively charged.

- At low pH, excess hydrogen ions promote protonation. The carboxyl group tends to remain as a carboxylic acid
- As pH rises, the carboxyl group loses its proton first
- At still higher pH, the protonated amino group loses its proton
- Ionisable side chains can alter the net charge and reactivity

### Side-chain classifications

| Class | Properties | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrophobic | Mostly hydrocarbon-containing. Tend to be buried inside soluble proteins | Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, proline, phenylalanine, tryptophan |
| Polar and uncharged | Contain electronegative atoms, can form hydrogen bonds | Serine, threonine, tyrosine, cysteine, asparagine, glutamine |
| Positively charged | Basic side chains, hydrophilic at physiological pH | Lysine, arginine, histidine |
| Negatively charged | Acidic side chains, commonly deprotonated at intracellular pH | Aspartate, glutamate |

### Four special amino acids

- **Glycine:** the smallest amino acid. Achiral because its side chain is a hydrogen atom
- **Proline:** has a ring connecting its side chain to the amino nitrogen, restricting backbone flexibility
- **Cysteine:** contains a thiol group. Two cysteine residues can form a covalent **disulfide bond**
- **Histidine:** can accept or donate a proton near physiological pH, so it is frequently found in **enzyme active sites**

Humans can synthesise some amino acids but must obtain **essential amino acids** from the diet.

## Protein Structure

### Peptide bonds

A peptide bond forms between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another in a **dehydration reaction**. Water is released and free energy is required for biosynthesis.

Sequences are conventionally written from the **amino terminus to the carboxyl terminus**.

The polypeptide backbone is repetitive, whereas the R groups provide chemical diversity. The peptide bond is planar and has partial double-bond character, restricting rotation. Rotation mainly occurs around the bonds adjacent to the peptide bond, allowing folding into many conformations.

### Four levels of protein structure

| Level | Description | Main stabilising features |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Linear amino-acid sequence | Peptide bonds. Sequence encoded by genetic information |
| Secondary | Local regular structures | Hydrogen bonds between backbone carbonyl and amino groups |
| Tertiary | Overall three-dimensional structure of one polypeptide | Hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, disulfide bonds |
| Quaternary | Arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits | Mostly non-covalent interactions, sometimes disulfide bonds |

### Secondary structures

- **Alpha helix:** a coiled structure stabilised by hydrogen bonding between backbone groups. The carbonyl group of one residue hydrogen-bonds with the amino group approximately four residues farther along the chain. Most biological alpha helices are right-handed
- **Beta sheet:** formed from extended beta strands connected by backbone hydrogen bonds. Strands may be parallel, antiparallel or mixed
- **Turns and loops:** allow the chain to reverse direction. Often occur on the protein surface

### Tertiary folding

In an aqueous environment, hydrophobic side chains generally become buried and polar or charged side chains become more exposed. This **hydrophobic effect** is a major driving force for folding. **Disulfide bridges** between cysteine residues provide particularly strong covalent stabilisation.

### Structure determines function

A change in amino-acid sequence can alter folding, binding, stability or catalytic activity.

- **Sickle-cell disease:** substitution of **glutamate by valine** in haemoglobin changes its properties and promotes formation of abnormal, rigid red blood cells
- **Cystic fibrosis:** deletion of a **phenylalanine** residue from the CFTR protein changes its primary structure and can alter tertiary structure and membrane-protein function

### Protein classifications

- **Fibrous proteins:** elongated structural proteins such as collagen, keratin, fibrin and some muscle proteins
- **Globular proteins:** compact folded proteins such as enzymes, myoglobin and haemoglobin
- **Functional groups:** enzymes, transport proteins, hormones, receptors, antibodies, contractile proteins, storage proteins

### Denaturation and renaturation

**Denaturation** is loss of the native conformation and function of a protein. Can result from heat, extreme pH, mechanical force, detergents, urea, or reducing agents that disrupt disulfide bonds.

Some proteins can **renature** when the original conditions are restored, demonstrating that the amino-acid sequence contains the information required for folding.

## Enzymes and Enzyme Kinetics

### Properties

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They increase reaction rates without being consumed. Highly specific for both the reaction and the substrate.

- Lower **activation energy** by providing an alternative pathway to the transition state
- Do **not** change the equilibrium position
- Do **not** change the overall free-energy change
- **Cannot** make a thermodynamically unfavourable reaction favourable
- Many require cofactors including metal ions or organic coenzymes

### Active sites and induced fit

The active site contains the substrate-binding region and catalytic residues. Substrate binding may cause a conformational change known as **induced fit**. Weak interactions such as hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces position the substrate correctly.

### Factors affecting activity

- **Enzyme concentration:** rate proportional to enzyme concentration when substrate is not limiting
- **Substrate concentration:** rate increases until the enzyme becomes saturated
- **Temperature:** increasing temperature generally increases rate until denaturation reduces activity
- **pH:** changes ionisation of catalytic residues and substrates, may disrupt protein structure
- **Cofactor availability:** required cofactors must be present
- **Inhibitors:** reduce catalytic activity

### Competitive vs non-competitive inhibition

| Feature | Competitive | Non-competitive (allosteric) |
|---|---|---|
| Binding site | Active site | Separate regulatory site |
| Mechanism | Inhibitor resembles substrate and blocks substrate binding | Inhibitor changes enzyme conformation and prevents effective catalysis |
| Effect of increasing substrate | May overcome inhibition | Usually does not overcome inhibition |
| Clinical example | Some drugs mimic substrates | Regulatory metabolites bind allosterically |

Inhibition can be reversible or irreversible. **Penicillin** irreversibly inhibits a bacterial enzyme required for cell-wall synthesis.

### Michaelis-Menten kinetics

For many enzymes, reaction velocity rises approximately hyperbolically as substrate concentration increases. At high substrate concentration the enzyme approaches a maximum velocity.

- **Vmax** is the maximum velocity when the enzyme is saturated
- **Km** is the substrate concentration at which velocity is half of Vmax
- A lower Km often indicates significant catalysis occurs at lower substrate concentration, although it is not a universal measure of binding affinity

The **Lineweaver-Burk** double-reciprocal plot estimates Km and Vmax from intercepts.

**Turnover number (kcat):** the number of substrate molecules converted per enzyme molecule per unit time when the enzyme is saturated.

### Allosteric enzymes

Commonly contain multiple subunits and active sites. Often produce **sigmoidal** rather than hyperbolic velocity-versus-substrate curves. Binding at one site can alter activity at another. Important pathway regulators.

## Carbohydrates

### General features

Carbon-based molecules rich in hydroxyl groups. Many have the empirical formula Cx(H2O)x. Function as energy sources, energy stores, structural materials, and components of nucleic acids, glycoproteins and glycolipids.

### Monosaccharides

Classified by carbon number: trioses, tetroses, pentoses, hexoses, heptoses. May contain an aldehyde group (**aldose**) or a ketone group (**ketose**). Glucose is an aldohexose, fructose is a ketohexose.

A **chiral carbon** is attached to four different groups. **Enantiomers** are mirror images, **epimers** differ at one chiral carbon. Most biological hexoses are D isomers.

In solution glucose cyclises. **Alpha and beta anomers** differ in the orientation of the hydroxyl group at the anomeric carbon. Interconversion is **mutarotation**. Cyclic aldohexoses are **pyranoses**, cyclic ketohexoses are **furanoses**.

### Disaccharides and polysaccharides

**Glycosidic bonds** join monosaccharides. Sucrose contains glucose and fructose. Maltose and lactose are other common disaccharides. Digestive enzymes hydrolyse complex carbohydrates to monosaccharides before absorption.

| Polymer | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Amylose | Mostly linear glucose polymer with alpha linkages | Plant energy storage |
| Amylopectin | Branched plant glucose polymer. Branches less frequent than glycogen | Plant energy storage |
| Glycogen | Highly branched animal glucose polymer | Animal energy storage in liver and muscle |
| Cellulose | Linear glucose polymer with beta linkages | Plant structural support. Dietary fibre for humans |

> Alpha linkages we can digest, beta linkages we cannot. That is why cellulose is fibre and starch is food.

## Glycolysis: The Ten Steps

### Preparatory phase

1. **Hexokinase** phosphorylates glucose, trapping it inside the cell
2. **Phosphoglucose isomerase** converts glucose phosphate to fructose phosphate
3. **Phosphofructokinase** phosphorylates the sugar a second time. Major regulatory step
4. **Aldolase** cleaves the six-carbon bisphosphate into two three-carbon phosphates
5. **Triose phosphate isomerase** converts the products into two glyceraldehyde phosphate

### Payoff phase

6. **Glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase** oxidises the substrate, producing reduced cofactor
7. **Phosphoglycerate kinase** transfers a phosphoryl group to ADP, producing ATP
8. **Phosphoglycerate mutase** rearranges the phosphoryl group
9. **Enolase** removes water, producing phosphoenolpyruvate
10. **Pyruvate kinase** transfers a phosphoryl group to ADP, producing ATP and pyruvate

Per glucose: uses 2 ATP, produces 4 ATP, **net gain 2 ATP**. Also produces 2 reduced cofactors and 2 pyruvate.

### Regulation

- Hexokinase inhibited by glucose phosphate
- Phosphofructokinase activated by low-energy signals (AMP, ADP), inhibited by ATP and high citrate
- Pyruvate kinase inhibited by ATP
- Insulin promotes glucose uptake and glycolysis
- Glucagon inhibits liver glycolysis through cyclic-AMP-dependent phosphorylation

## Tricarboxylic Acid Cycle

### Link reaction

Pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix through a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier. The **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex** irreversibly converts pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, CO2 and reduced cofactor. The complex contains several enzymes and cofactors and enables substrate channeling.

### The eight steps

1. Acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate
2. Citrate isomerised to isocitrate
3. Isocitrate oxidatively decarboxylated to alpha-ketoglutarate
4. Alpha-ketoglutarate oxidatively decarboxylated to succinyl-CoA
5. Succinyl-CoA converted to succinate with substrate-level phosphorylation
6. Succinate oxidised to fumarate, producing reduced flavin cofactor
7. Fumarate hydrated to malate
8. Malate oxidised to oxaloacetate

Per acetyl-CoA: 2 CO2, 3 reduced nicotinamide cofactors, 1 reduced flavin cofactor, 1 ATP equivalent.

### Functions

- Oxidises acetyl units to CO2
- Produces reduced cofactors for oxidative phosphorylation
- Acts as the **common final pathway** for carbohydrate, fatty-acid and amino-acid catabolism
- Provides intermediates for amino-acid, nucleotide, haem and lipid synthesis

The cycle does not directly use molecular oxygen but depends on it indirectly, because oxidised cofactors must be regenerated through the respiratory chain.

### Regulation

High ATP and reduced cofactors inhibit major dehydrogenases. ADP and substrate availability promote flux. If intermediates are removed for biosynthesis, **anaplerotic reactions** replenish them. Pyruvate carboxylase converts pyruvate to oxaloacetate.

## Oxidative Phosphorylation

### Electron transport chain

| Complex | Function | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Complex I | NADH-coenzyme Q reductase | Accepts electrons from NADH through FMN and iron-sulfur centres. Reduces coenzyme Q. Pumps protons |
| Complex II | Succinate-coenzyme Q reductase | Accepts electrons from reduced flavin cofactor, transfers to coenzyme Q. **Does not pump protons** |
| Complex III | Coenzyme Q-cytochrome c reductase | Transfers electrons to cytochrome c through the Q cycle. Pumps protons |
| Complex IV | Cytochrome c oxidase | Transfers electrons to oxygen, forming water. Pumps protons |

**Coenzyme Q** is a lipid-soluble mobile carrier within the inner membrane. **Cytochrome c** is a water-soluble carrier on the outer surface of the inner membrane.

### Chemiosmosis

Electron transfer releases energy incrementally. Complexes pump protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical proton gradient. The inner membrane must remain relatively impermeable so the gradient is maintained.

Protons flow back into the matrix through **ATP synthase**, driving rotation and conformational changes that phosphorylate ADP.

### ATP synthase

- **F0:** membrane-embedded proton channel and rotary component
- **F1:** matrix-facing catalytic component containing beta subunits
- **Open state:** ATP released
- **Loose state:** ADP and inorganic phosphate bind
- **Tight state:** ATP formed

Approximate yields: **2.5 ATP per NADH**, **1.5 ATP per reduced flavin cofactor**. Complete aerobic oxidation of one glucose yields approximately **32 ATP**, depending on the cytosolic NADH shuttle.

### Shuttles and regulation

Cytosolic NADH cannot normally cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. Shuttle systems transfer its electrons. Some deliver at Complex I (higher ATP yield), others at a lower level (lower yield).

Oxidative phosphorylation is controlled by **ATP demand**. Electron flow accelerates when ADP and inorganic phosphate are available. This is **respiratory control by acceptor availability**.

### Inhibitors and uncouplers

- **Rotenone** inhibits Complex I
- **Antimycin** inhibits Complex III
- **Cyanide** inhibits Complex IV and prevents oxygen reduction
- **Uncouplers** carry protons across the inner membrane, dissipating the gradient. Electron transport continues but ATP production falls or stops and energy is released as heat
