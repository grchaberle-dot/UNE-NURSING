# Autonomic Nervous System

**Sources:** University of Queensland BIOM2012, QUT LSB142 (Studocu), Queensland Spinal Cord Injuries Service (qscis.health.qld.gov.au), UQ Queensland Brain Institute (qbi.uq.edu.au). All Australian.

---

## What the ANS Controls

Controls involuntary functions to maintain homeostasis: heart rate, digestion, breathing, glandular secretion, cardiovascular activity. Regulated by the **hypothalamus**.

ANS motor output targets **smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands**. Not skeletal muscle, which is somatic.

The ANS is required for cardiac function, respiration and reflexes including vomiting, coughing and sneezing.

---

## ANS vs Somatic Nervous System

| Feature | Somatic NS | Autonomic NS |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Voluntary, conscious | Involuntary, automatic |
| Primary targets | Skeletal muscles | Smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands |
| Motor pathway | One motor neuron (CNS to skeletal muscle) | Two-neuron chain: preganglionic, ganglion, postganglionic |
| Synapse outside CNS | None | Present in autonomic ganglion |
| Main neurotransmitter | ACh at skeletal muscle | ACh (parasympathetic) or noradrenaline (sympathetic) |

## Three Divisions

### 1. Sympathetic (fight or flight)

**Origin:** thoracolumbar spinal cord, **T1 to L2**. Preganglionic neuron cell bodies in the lateral horns of thoracic and upper lumbar cord.

**Sympathetic chain:** paired elongated structures anterolateral to the vertebral column, containing chain ganglia where preganglionic neurons synapse with postganglionic neurons.

**Pathway:** short preganglionic axon -> ganglion close to the spinal cord -> long postganglionic axon -> target

**Postganglionic neurotransmitter:** primarily **noradrenaline**. Some use ACh (sweat glands), some use dopamine (renal).

- **Fight response:** increased heart rate, adrenaline release, vasoconstriction to redirect blood where needed most
- **Flight response:** inhibits digestion and urination

**Key actions:** increases heart rate and contractility, dilates pupils, bronchodilation, inhibits GI motility, contracts sphincters, redirects blood flow to skeletal muscles and brain and heart, activates sweat glands, relaxes urinary bladder, adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline and noradrenaline, reduces exocrine pancreatic secretion and inhibits insulin.

Sweat glands, most blood vessels and some glands receive **sympathetic innervation only**.

### 2. Parasympathetic (rest and digest)

**Origin:** craniosacral. Preganglionic cell bodies in the brainstem (cranial nerves) plus sacral spinal cord **S2 to S4**.

**Pathway:** long preganglionic axon -> ganglion close to or within the target organ -> short postganglionic axon -> target

**Postganglionic neurotransmitter:** **acetylcholine**.

Parasympathetic motor fibres are found in four of the twelve pairs of cranial nerves.

**Key actions:** decreases heart rate and contractility, constricts pupils, bronchoconstriction, increases GI motility and secretions, relaxes sphincters, contracts bladder, facilitates erection, contracts gallbladder, increases exocrine pancreatic secretion, large volume watery saliva, promotes energy storage.

### 3. Enteric (the second brain)

Controls GI functions: gastrointestinal movements, gastric acid secretion, local blood flow, gut hormone release, interaction with the gut immune system.

## Organ-by-Organ Comparison

| Organ or function | Parasympathetic effect | Sympathetic effect |
|---|---|---|
| Brain | Not specified | Increases alertness, may improve memory and problem solving |
| Pupils | Constriction, allowing better focus | Dilation, increasing the visual field |
| Heart | Reduces rate and force of contraction | Increases rate and force, delivering more blood to brain, heart and skeletal muscle |
| Airways | Bronchoconstriction, reducing air intake | Bronchodilation, increasing air intake |
| GI tract | Increases motility, relaxes sphincters, increases digestive enzyme secretion | Reduces motility, contracts sphincters |
| Urinary bladder | Contracts the bladder to facilitate emptying | Relaxes the bladder |
| Genital organs | Facilitates erection by dilating blood vessels | Controls genital activity except during orgasm |
| Blood flow | Constricts vessels supplying GI tract, bladder and genitals | Increases flow to skeletal muscle, brain and heart. Constricts GI, bladder and genital vessels |
| Gallbladder | Contracts, aiding emptying | Relaxes |
| Pancreas | Increases exocrine secretion | Reduces exocrine secretion, inhibits insulin secretion |
| Sweat glands | Not generally parasympathetically controlled | Increases secretion |
| Salivary glands | Large volume of watery saliva | Small volume of thick saliva |
| Adrenal medulla | Not a major target | Secretes adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood |

**Cardiac output** depends on stroke volume and heart rate.

### Important exceptions

Most blood vessels, most sweat glands and salivary glands are exceptions to the usual pattern of dual control.

- **Exocrine glands** maintain contact with the body surface through a duct (tear, sweat, mammary glands)
- **Endocrine glands** release hormones directly into the blood, with no ducts
- The **liver, gonads and pancreas** have both exocrine and endocrine functions

## General ANS Circuitry

1. A visceral receptor detects mechanical strain or chemical changes in an organ
2. An afferent neuron carries sensory information through the dorsal side of the spinal cord
3. Information reaches autonomic motor neurons directly or via interneurons
4. An autonomic motor neuron carries the signal through the ventral side of the cord
5. The preganglionic fibre enters an autonomic ganglion and forms synaptic boutons on ganglion cells
6. The ganglion processes and passes the signal through a postganglionic fibre
7. The postganglionic fibre communicates with visceral effector cells (smooth muscle or secretory cells)
8. The effector produces the appropriate response

**Autonomic ganglia** connect central motor neurons with effector organs. They contain circuits that integrate and regulate neural signals before those signals reach the neuroeffector junction. Ganglia are not merely relay points: they can amplify, inhibit, diverge or converge neural information.

## Neurotransmitter Pathways

### Shared preganglionic pathway

Both divisions: preganglionic neurons release **ACh**, acting on **nicotinic receptors** on ganglion cells.

### Parasympathetic postganglionic

Releases **ACh** via varicosities, acting on **muscarinic receptors** on the target organ.

### Sympathetic postganglionic

Primarily releases **noradrenaline**, acting on **adrenergic receptors**. ATP may be co-released with noradrenaline (faster contraction component). Exceptions: some sympathetic neurons are cholinergic (sweat glands, certain blood vessels) or dopaminergic (renal).

### Sympathetic neuroendocrine activity

Some sympathetic fibres directly innervate **chromaffin cells** in the adrenal medulla, causing secretion of adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood. This produces body-wide broadcasting of sympathetic activation rather than a local response.

## Cholinergic Transmission

1. Choline transported into the nerve ending
2. Choline converted into ACh
3. ACh stored in vesicles near the nerve ending
4. An action potential stimulates the nerve terminal
5. Calcium channels open, Ca2+ enters the terminal
6. Calcium activates vesicle movement toward the membrane
7. Vesicles fuse and release ACh into the synaptic cleft
8. ACh binds receptors on the target cell, producing a response
9. ACh can also act on the nerve-terminal membrane to inhibit further release
10. Remaining ACh broken down into choline and acetate
11. Choline recycled into the nerve terminal for new ACh synthesis

### Cholinergic receptor types

| Receptor | Type | Location and features |
|---|---|---|
| Nicotinic (Nm) | Ion channel | Neuromuscular junction |
| Nicotinic (Nn) | Ion channel | Autonomic ganglion cells, CNS |
| Muscarinic M1 | G-protein coupled | Mainly CNS |
| Muscarinic M2 | G-protein coupled | Cardiac muscle. Inhibitory signalling |
| Muscarinic M3 | G-protein coupled | GI smooth muscle and glands. Stimulatory signalling |

**Nicotinic:** ion channels stimulated by ACh or nicotine. When activated they are permeable to ions, producing rapid depolarisation.

**Muscarinic:** G-protein coupled. Activation can stimulate or inhibit the target cell. Five subtypes (M1 to M5).

## Adrenergic Transmission

1. Tyrosine enters the nerve terminal
2. Tyrosine converted into dopamine, then into noradrenaline
3. Noradrenaline stored in vesicles
4. An action potential activates calcium channels
5. Ca2+ enters the nerve ending
6. Calcium causes vesicles to move to and fuse with the membrane
7. Noradrenaline released into the synaptic cleft
8. Noradrenaline binds adrenergic receptors on the effector organ
9. Noradrenaline also acts on nerve-terminal receptors, providing negative feedback

### Adrenergic receptor types

| Receptor | Key effect or location |
|---|---|
| Alpha-1 | Smooth muscle contraction, vasoconstriction in the GI tract, reduced gut blood flow |
| Alpha-2 | CNS. Involved in inhibitory negative feedback |
| Beta-1 | Increases cardiac muscle contractility |
| Beta-2 | Vasodilation in skeletal-muscle vasculature, bronchodilation |

**Alpha receptors** have greater affinity for noradrenaline. **Beta receptors** have greater affinity for adrenaline.

### Mechanisms in smooth muscle

Noradrenaline binding to an alpha-1 receptor activates a G-protein-coupled pathway, increasing calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum and causing contraction. This does not require an ionic change across the cell membrane because the calcium is released internally.

ATP can bind receptors which are ion channels. Activation allows Ca2+ entry, depolarises the membrane and produces contraction. ATP produces a rapid contraction through depolarisation, while noradrenaline produces a smaller, longer-lasting component.

## Smooth Muscle Innervation

| Type | Features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Single-unit | Cells connected by gap junctions containing connexins. Electrical signals spread cell to cell. Not every cell is directly innervated but they contract as one unit | GI tract |
| Multi-unit | Cells not linked by gap junctions. Each cell must be individually innervated | Eye muscles |

## Boutons vs Varicosities

- **Synaptic boutons:** small swellings at the terminal ends of axons where synapses occur. In autonomic ganglia, preganglionic axons form boutons on ganglion cells. Release ACh, activating nicotinic receptors
- **Varicosities:** swellings distributed **along** the axon rather than restricted to one terminal ending. Contain neurotransmitter packets and mitochondria. Release transmitter onto effector cells. Parasympathetic varicosities release ACh. Sympathetic varicosities primarily release noradrenaline and may also release ATP

## ANS Disruption in Spinal Cord Injury

**Source: Queensland Spinal Cord Injuries Service (Queensland Health)**

Spinal cord injury can disrupt the connection between brain and autonomic pathways, causing imbalance. Effects depend on the level of damage.

Because the sympathetic nervous system originates T1 to L2, **injuries at or above T6** can severely disrupt sympathetic function.

### Consequences of ANS disruption after SCI

- **Autonomic dysreflexia:** a medical emergency characterised by a sudden rise in systolic blood pressure
- **Cardiac issues:** including arrhythmias
- **Systemic hypotension:** low blood pressure
- **Respiratory problems:** bronchoconstriction and increased lung secretions
- **Bowel and bladder control:** loss of voluntary control
- **Exaggerated reflexes:** uncontrolled responses to stimuli
- **Sexual dysfunction**
- **Temperature regulation issues**

## UQ Queensland Brain Institute Notes

The ANS is also called the vegetative nervous system.

- Sympathetic fibres use **noradrenaline** to activate blood flow in skeletal muscles and lungs, dilate lungs and blood vessels, and raise heart rate. Synapses are **excitatory**
- Parasympathetic fibres regulate resting responses: heart rate, salivation, lacrimation, digestion. The only exception is sexual arousal. Synapses are typically **inhibitory** with ACh as the main neurotransmitter
- Although most ANS responses are involuntary, they integrate with the somatic nervous system. Defecation involves interplay between voluntary and involuntary movements
