# GI & Liver Emergencies

**Unit:** HSNS363  
**Module ID:** `giLiverEmergencies`

> Manage acute pancreatitis, liver cirrhosis with portal hypertension, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy and upper GI haemorrhage.

---

## Learn

### Pancreas: exocrine and endocrine roles

- **Exocrine function:** Acinar cells produce digestive enzymes, including amylase, lipase and proteases, which are released into the duodenum.
- **Endocrine function:** The islets release hormones such as insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose.
- **Clinical relevance:** Acute inflammation can impair digestion, glucose regulation and systemic physiology; monitor both abdominal and metabolic effects.

### Acute pancreatitis: causes

- **Common causes:** Alcohol use and gallstones are major causes of acute pancreatitis.
- **Obstruction:** A gallstone can obstruct the pancreatic or common bile duct, disrupting enzyme drainage and contributing to pancreatic injury.
- **Other considerations:** Review the history for procedures, medicines, metabolic triggers, trauma and other causes identified by the treating team.

### Acute pancreatitis pathophysiology

- **1. Obstruction or injury:** Duct obstruction or acinar-cell injury disrupts normal enzyme handling.
- **2. Premature activation:** Digestive enzymes, especially trypsin, become activated inside the pancreas rather than in the intestine.
- **3. Autodigestion:** Activated enzymes digest pancreatic tissue, causing inflammation, oedema and cellular injury.
- **4. SIRS:** Inflammatory mediators enter the systemic circulation and can produce systemic inflammatory response syndrome.
- **5. Organ dysfunction:** Severe inflammation, capillary leak and third spacing can impair circulation and distant organs.
- **6. Multiorgan dysfunction:** Severe acute pancreatitis may progress to respiratory, renal, cardiovascular or other multiorgan dysfunction.

### Pancreatitis severity

- **Mild:** Acute pancreatitis without organ failure or local or systemic complications; it often resolves with supportive care.
- **Moderate:** Transient organ failure or local or systemic complications may occur but resolve within the expected timeframe.
- **Severe:** Persistent organ failure, commonly involving respiratory, cardiovascular or renal systems, indicates severe disease and high risk.
- **Nursing focus:** Trend observations, pain, perfusion, oxygenation, urine output, fluid balance and laboratory results to detect deterioration early.

### Assessing acute pancreatitis

- **Symptoms:** Assess severe upper abdominal pain, radiation to the back, nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension and inability to tolerate intake.
- **Circulation and breathing:** Check airway, respiratory effort, oxygenation, heart rate, blood pressure, perfusion, temperature and mental state.
- **Laboratory tests:** Review ordered pancreatic enzymes, full blood count, electrolytes, renal function, glucose, calcium, liver tests and inflammatory markers.
- **Imaging and output:** CT may assess complications; monitor urine output, fluid balance, weight and abdominal findings.

### Initial management of pancreatitis

- **ABC and escalation:** Perform an ABCDE assessment, recognise SIRS or organ dysfunction and escalate early.
- **IV fluids:** Prescribed IV fluid resuscitation supports circulating volume and perfusion; reassess for response and fluid overload.
- **Pain management:** Administer prescribed analgesia and antiemetics, then reassess pain, respiratory status, sedation and function.
- **NBM and nutrition:** Keep the patient nil by mouth (NBM) when ordered; nutritional support is planned according to severity and gastrointestinal function.
- **Monitoring:** Monitor BGL, electrolytes, renal function, fluid balance, observations and signs of local or systemic complications.

### Pancreatitis investigations and supportive devices

- **CT:** CT may identify pancreatic inflammation, necrosis, collections or other complications when clinically indicated.
- **NGT:** A nasogastric tube may be used for persistent vomiting, ileus or decompression when prescribed; maintain tube safety and document drainage.
- **Antibiotics:** Antibiotics are not automatically required for uncomplicated acute pancreatitis; administer when prescribed for a suspected or confirmed infection.
- **BGL:** Pancreatic endocrine injury and physiological stress can disturb glucose control, so monitor BGL and treat as ordered.

### Liver cirrhosis: definition and function

- **Definition:** Cirrhosis is progressive, usually irreversible fibrosis and architectural distortion of the liver caused by repeated injury.
- **Metabolism:** The liver metabolises nutrients, hormones and medicines and helps regulate energy stores.
- **Detoxification and bile:** It detoxifies or transforms substances and produces bile for fat digestion and waste excretion.
- **Proteins and immunity:** It produces albumin and clotting factors, participates in immune defence and stores vitamins, minerals and glycogen.

### Cirrhosis pathophysiology

- **Repeated injury:** Chronic alcohol-related, viral, metabolic, autoimmune or other injury activates inflammatory pathways.
- **Stellate cells:** Hepatic stellate cells become activated and produce excess collagen and extracellular matrix.
- **Fibrosis:** Collagen deposition forms fibrous bands and regenerative nodules, distorting normal hepatic architecture and blood flow.
- **Portal hypertension:** Resistance to portal blood flow increases portal venous pressure and drives collateral circulation and ascites.
- **Reduced function:** Loss of functioning hepatocytes reduces metabolism, detoxification, albumin and clotting-factor synthesis and immune function.

### Portal hypertension and varices

- **Portal hypertension:** Fibrosis and vascular resistance raise pressure in the portal venous system.
- **Collateral vessels:** Blood is redirected through fragile collateral veins, including oesophageal and gastric varices.
- **Variceal risk:** Varices can rupture and cause rapid, life-threatening upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage.
- **Assessment:** Monitor for haematemesis, melaena, shock, falling haemoglobin, tachycardia, hypotension and altered consciousness.

### Ascites: mechanism and assessment

- **Mechanism:** Ascites develops through portal hypertension, low albumin and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which promotes sodium and water retention.
- **Clinical signs:** Look for increasing abdominal girth, shifting dullness, discomfort, early satiety, oedema and breathlessness from raised intra-abdominal pressure.
- **Complications:** Ascites can be complicated by infection, respiratory compromise, malnutrition, skin breakdown and hepatorenal dysfunction.
- **Monitoring:** Measure daily weight and abdominal girth as ordered, record fluid balance and assess urine output, breathing and skin.

### Ascites management

- **Fluid and sodium:** Monitor fluid balance and prescribe sodium restriction; fluid restriction may be ordered in selected patients.
- **Diuretics:** Prescribed diuretics promote sodium and fluid loss; monitor weight, renal function, electrolytes, blood pressure and encephalopathy.
- **Paracentesis:** Therapeutic paracentesis removes ascitic fluid when tense or refractory ascites causes symptoms; follow aseptic and post-procedure monitoring requirements.
- **Nursing care:** Provide skin care, support mobility and breathing, assess for infection and escalate increasing pain, fever, hypotension or reduced urine output.

### Hepatic encephalopathy

- **Mechanism:** Reduced hepatic detoxification allows ammonia and other neurotoxins to accumulate and affect brain function.
- **Features:** Confusion, altered sleep, personality or behaviour change, reduced consciousness, slurred speech and asterixis may occur.
- **Lactulose:** Lactulose acidifies colonic contents and traps ammonia as ammonium, increasing faecal excretion; titrate to the prescribed bowel-movement goal.
- **Neomycin:** Neomycin may reduce ammonia-producing gut bacteria when prescribed, but monitor for medicine-specific adverse effects.
- **Nursing priorities:** Assess neurological status, falls and aspiration risk, identify triggers such as infection or bleeding, administer treatment and escalate deterioration.

### Upper gastrointestinal bleeding: causes and priorities

- **Causes:** Varices, peptic ulcer disease and Mallory-Weiss tears are important causes of upper GI haemorrhage.
- **Airway:** Protect the airway in massive haematemesis, reduced consciousness or ongoing aspiration risk; call for urgent help.
- **Circulation:** Insert two large-bore IV cannulae, take ordered blood samples including crossmatch, monitor observations and assess shock.
- **Bleeding signs:** Haematemesis, coffee-ground vomit, melaena, tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, cool skin and confusion indicate possible significant blood loss.

### Upper GI bleeding: treatment and nursing care

- **Resuscitation:** Support airway, breathing and circulation, administer prescribed fluids or blood products and reassess response frequently.
- **Medicines:** A proton-pump inhibitor (PPI) may treat suspected ulcer bleeding; octreotide reduces splanchnic blood flow in suspected variceal bleeding when prescribed.
- **Endoscopy:** Urgent endoscopy identifies and treats the bleeding source, including endoscopic haemostasis or variceal band ligation.
- **Preparation:** Keep the patient NBM as ordered, maintain IV access, document losses, monitor urine output and prepare for theatre, interventional radiology or critical care if needed.
- **Safety:** Avoid oral intake during active bleeding unless directed, monitor for rebleeding and communicate trends promptly.

**Q10.** Oesophageal varices are fragile collateral veins associated with portal hypertension.

- Answer: **True** - Portal hypertension redirects blood through collateral vessels such as oesophageal varices, which can rupture and bleed severely.
