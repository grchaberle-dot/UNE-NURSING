## Learn

### Pulmonary embolism: definition and embolus types

- **Definition:** A pulmonary embolism (PE) is an obstruction of a pulmonary artery or one of its branches by an embolus, most commonly a thrombus that has travelled from a deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
- **Thrombus:** A clot formed in a vein, often in the deep veins of the leg or pelvis, can dislodge and travel to the pulmonary circulation.
- **Other emboli:** Less common emboli include fat, air or gas, amniotic fluid and septic material.
- **Urgency:** A large or multiple emboli can rapidly impair gas exchange, strain the right ventricle and cause obstructive shock or death.

### PE pathophysiology: the seven-step chain

- **1. Entry:** An embolus enters the venous circulation, usually after forming in a deep vein.
- **2. Lodgement:** It travels through the right heart and lodges in a pulmonary artery.
- **3. V/Q mismatch:** Ventilation continues to areas that are no longer perfused, producing ventilation-perfusion mismatch and increased dead space.
- **4. Hypoxaemia:** Impaired gas exchange and reduced effective perfusion lower arterial oxygenation.
- **5. Vasoconstriction:** Pulmonary vasoconstriction increases pulmonary vascular resistance.
- **6. RV strain:** The right ventricle must pump against the increased afterload; it can dilate and fail.
- **7. Shock or death:** Reduced left-sided filling and cardiac output may progress to hypotension, obstructive shock and death.

### Recognising PE

- **Respiratory:** Sudden dyspnoea, tachypnoea and hypoxaemia are common; the severity depends on clot burden and reserve.
- **Pain:** Pleuritic chest pain may occur, particularly when a peripheral pulmonary infarct irritates the pleura.
- **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardia, hypotension, syncope or signs of shock suggest significant obstruction and right-heart strain.
- **Other:** Haemoptysis may occur. A patient can have a PE without obvious leg signs, so maintain suspicion in an at-risk patient.

### Virchow's triad

- **Venous stasis:** Immobility, prolonged travel, hospitalisation, paralysis and heart failure slow venous flow.
- **Hypercoagulability:** Malignancy, pregnancy or the postpartum period, oestrogen therapy, inherited thrombophilia and dehydration can increase clotting tendency.
- **Endothelial injury:** Trauma, surgery, vascular disease and an indwelling vascular device can damage the vessel lining.
- **Clinical link:** The more components present, the greater the risk of DVT and subsequent PE; assess risk and prevention opportunities proactively.

### Investigating suspected PE

- **CTPA:** CT pulmonary angiography is commonly used to visualise a filling defect in the pulmonary arterial tree, when appropriate for the patient.
- **ABG:** An arterial blood gas assesses oxygenation, ventilation and acid-base status; it may show hypoxaemia but is not diagnostic alone.
- **ECG:** An ECG helps assess tachycardia, right-heart strain and alternative cardiac causes; a normal ECG does not exclude PE.
- **CXR:** A chest X-ray helps exclude other causes of acute respiratory symptoms and is often non-specific in PE.
- **Blood tests:** Troponin can indicate myocardial strain or injury; coagulation studies establish baseline clotting status and support anticoagulant management.

### Immediate nursing management of PE

- **Primary assessment:** Use an urgent ABCDE assessment, recognise deterioration and escalate promptly according to local rapid-response policy.
- **Airway and oxygen:** Assess airway patency and administer prescribed oxygen, titrating to the ordered target saturation and monitoring response.
- **Rest and positioning:** Keep the patient at rest, usually in a position that supports breathing, and avoid unnecessary exertion.
- **Monitoring:** Continuously or frequently monitor respiratory effort, SpO2, heart rate, blood pressure, level of consciousness and rhythm as indicated.
- **Access and preparation:** Obtain IV access, collect ordered blood tests, prepare for imaging and treatment, and assess bleeding risk before anticoagulation.

### PE treatment options

- **Anticoagulation:** Heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) may be used to prevent clot extension and recurrence.
- **Thrombolysis:** Alteplase may be considered for life-threatening or haemodynamically unstable PE when benefits outweigh bleeding risk.
- **Embolectomy:** A clot may be removed surgically or by catheter in selected severe cases when other treatment is unsuitable or unsuccessful.
- **IVC filter:** An inferior vena cava filter can reduce embolisation from lower-limb veins when anticoagulation is contraindicated or ineffective; it does not dissolve an existing PE.
- **Nursing safety:** Check prescribed dosing, renal function, recent bleeding, procedures, platelet count and relevant coagulation results; monitor for bleeding.

### Acute pulmonary oedema: definition and causes

- **Definition:** Acute pulmonary oedema (APO) is life-threatening accumulation of fluid in the pulmonary interstitium and alveoli, impairing oxygen diffusion.
- **Left ventricular failure:** When the left ventricle cannot eject effectively, pressure is transmitted backwards into the pulmonary circulation.
- **Acute myocardial infarction:** An AMI can abruptly reduce left-ventricular contractility and precipitate APO.
- **Other triggers:** Dysrhythmia, acute valvular disease and fluid overload can raise cardiac filling pressures and cause APO.

### Cardiogenic APO: the six-step chain

- **1. LV failure:** The left ventricle fails to pump blood forward effectively.
- **2. Back-up:** Blood backs up into the left atrium and pulmonary veins.
- **3. Hydrostatic pressure:** Pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure rises.
- **4. Fluid movement:** Fluid moves from capillaries into the interstitium and then the alveoli.
- **5. Surfactant washout:** Fluid dilutes or washes out surfactant, reducing alveolar stability and compliance.
- **6. Hypoxaemia:** Fluid-filled alveoli cannot exchange gases effectively, causing severe hypoxaemia and increased work of breathing.

### Recognising acute pulmonary oedema

- **Breathing:** Extreme breathlessness, tachypnoea, orthopnoea, inability to speak comfortably and widespread inspiratory crackles may occur.
- **Secretions:** Pink, frothy sputum is a classic sign of alveolar fluid, although it may not be present.
- **Oxygenation:** Cyanosis, falling SpO2, agitation or exhaustion indicate severe impairment and possible impending respiratory failure.
- **Circulation:** Tachycardia and a raised JVP may reflect cardiac failure; blood pressure may be high initially or fall with severe decompensation.

### Urgent APO management

- **Airway and position:** Assess airway, call for urgent assistance and sit the patient upright with legs dependent if tolerated to reduce venous return.
- **Oxygen:** Administer prescribed oxygen and titrate to the ordered target; reassess work of breathing, mental state and SpO2.
- **NIPPV:** Non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation can recruit alveoli, improve oxygenation and reduce the work of breathing when appropriate.
- **Vasodilation:** Prescribed glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) reduces preload and afterload when blood pressure permits; monitor closely for hypotension.
- **Diuresis:** Prescribed furosemide promotes diuresis; monitor urine output, blood pressure, renal function and electrolytes.
- **Monitoring:** Continuous ECG and frequent vital-sign assessment are needed, with IV access and early escalation for deterioration.

### NIPPV: CPAP and BiPAP

- **CPAP:** Continuous positive airway pressure provides one continuous level of pressure throughout inspiration and expiration, helping splint alveoli open.
- **BiPAP:** Bilevel positive airway pressure provides a higher inspiratory pressure and a lower expiratory pressure, assisting ventilation as well as oxygenation.
- **Indications:** NIPPV may be indicated for cardiogenic APO with respiratory distress, hypoxaemia or increased work of breathing when the patient can protect their airway.
- **How it helps:** Positive pressure recruits alveoli, improves functional residual capacity, reduces preload and afterload, and decreases the work of breathing.
- **Teamwork:** Use the prescribed settings and local protocol; involve senior clinicians and prepare for intubation if response is inadequate.

### NIPPV contraindications and complications

- **Contraindications:** Avoid or use extreme caution with inability to protect the airway, reduced consciousness, vomiting or high aspiration risk, facial trauma, an unsuitable mask or haemodynamic instability.
- **Complications:** Mask leak, pressure injury, anxiety or claustrophobia, gastric distension, hypotension, aspiration and delayed recognition of respiratory failure can occur.
- **Nursing care:** Explain and reassure, check mask fit and skin, assess synchrony and secretions, monitor observations closely and provide breaks only when safe.
- **Escalation:** Worsening fatigue, hypoxaemia, altered consciousness or inability to tolerate the mask requires immediate review and preparation for invasive support.

### High-flow nasal oxygen

- **Physiological effects:** Heated, humidified high-flow oxygen delivers a reliable high inspired oxygen concentration, reduces entrainment of room air, provides low-level positive pressure and washes out nasopharyngeal dead space.
- **Indications:** It can support hypoxaemic respiratory failure, reduce work of breathing and provide oxygen when conventional nasal cannulae are insufficient, according to local protocol.
- **Nursing care:** Check prescribed flow and FiO2, humidification, cannula position, comfort, skin integrity, respiratory effort and oxygen saturation; monitor for drying, condensation and intolerance.
- **Safety:** High-flow oxygen is supportive, not a substitute for airway protection. Escalate promptly if oxygenation or clinical work of breathing worsens.

## Practice (10 questions)

**Q1.** Which sequence best describes the main pathophysiology of a pulmonary embolism?

- Alveolar fluid -> LV failure -> bradycardia -> hypoxaemia
- **Embolus lodges in a pulmonary artery -> V/Q mismatch -> hypoxaemia -> pulmonary vasoconstriction and RV strain** (correct)
- Bronchospasm -> pulmonary oedema -> increased venous return -> RV recovery
- Hyperventilation -> increased perfusion -> reduced pulmonary resistance -> shock

**Q2.** Match each component of Virchow's triad with an example.

- Venous stasis -> Prolonged immobility or hospitalisation
- Hypercoagulability -> Malignancy or pregnancy
- Endothelial injury -> Surgery, trauma or vascular-device damage

**Q3.** A normal ECG rules out pulmonary embolism.

- Answer: **False** - An ECG may show tachycardia or right-heart strain and helps assess alternatives, but a normal ECG does not exclude PE.

**Q4.** Put the PE pathophysiology sequence in the correct order.

1. Embolus enters the venous circulation
2. Embolus lodges in a pulmonary artery
3. Ventilation-perfusion mismatch develops
4. Hypoxaemia occurs
5. Pulmonary vasoconstriction increases resistance
6. Right-ventricular strain develops
7. Obstructive shock or death may follow

**Q5.** Which finding most strongly suggests acute pulmonary oedema?

- Unilateral calf bruising only
- **Pink, frothy sputum with widespread crackles and severe breathlessness** (correct)
- Slow pulse with dry mucous membranes
- Isolated pleuritic pain with a normal respiratory rate

**Q6.** Match each APO intervention with its intended effect.

- Sit upright -> Reduces venous return and supports breathing
- GTN -> Reduces preload and afterload when blood pressure permits
- Furosemide -> Promotes diuresis
- NIPPV -> Recruits alveoli and reduces work of breathing

**Q7.** BiPAP provides a higher inspiratory pressure and a lower expiratory pressure.

- Answer: **True** - BiPAP is bilevel support: inspiratory positive airway pressure is higher than expiratory pressure.

**Q8.** Which patient is least suitable for NIPPV without urgent senior review?

- An alert patient with cardiogenic APO who can protect their airway
- A patient with increased work of breathing who follows commands
- **A vomiting patient with reduced consciousness and a high aspiration risk** (correct)
- A cooperative patient with hypoxaemia despite conventional oxygen

**Q9.** Put the immediate nursing priorities for a deteriorating patient with APO in a sensible order.

1. Recognise deterioration and call for urgent assistance
2. Assess airway, breathing and circulation
3. Sit the patient upright and administer prescribed oxygen
4. Apply monitoring and obtain IV access
5. Prepare prescribed NIPPV and medications while reassessing response
6. Escalate for invasive airway support if the patient worsens

**Q10.** An IVC filter dissolves an existing pulmonary embolus.

- Answer: **False** - An IVC filter can reduce embolisation from lower-limb veins when anticoagulation is unsuitable; it does not dissolve an existing PE.
