# Chronic Kidney Disease & Dialysis

**Unit:** HSNS363

## Learn

### Chronic kidney disease: definition and causes

- **Definition:** Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is kidney damage or reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) that persists for at least three months.
- **Common causes:** Diabetes, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, cardiovascular disease, urinary obstruction, hereditary disease and unresolved acute kidney injury can cause CKD.
- **Silent progression:** Nephron loss can progress gradually with few symptoms; assessment and risk-factor management are important before advanced disease develops.

### CKD stages 1 to 5

- **Stage 1:** Normal or high GFR with evidence of kidney damage. Identify the cause, monitor kidney function and reduce risks.
- **Stage 2:** Mildly reduced GFR with kidney damage. Continue monitoring and manage causes and cardiovascular risk.
- **Stage 3:** Moderately reduced GFR. Assess complications such as anaemia, mineral-bone disorder and acidosis; slow further decline.
- **Stage 4:** Severely reduced GFR. Plan renal replacement therapy, educate the patient and manage complications closely.
- **Stage 5:** Kidney failure or end-stage renal failure. Renal replacement therapy or conservative management may be required according to goals and clinical need.

### Reduced excretion: uraemia and fluid problems

- **Waste excretion:** Reduced filtration and waste excretion cause accumulation of uraemic toxins, producing fatigue, anorexia, nausea, pruritus, confusion and other uraemic features.
- **Sodium and water:** Impaired regulation promotes fluid retention, oedema and hypertension; advanced disease may progress from polyuria to oliguria.
- **Proteinuria:** Damage to the glomerular filtration barrier allows protein to enter the urine; persistent proteinuria is both a marker and contributor to kidney disease.
- **Platelet dysfunction:** Uraemia can impair platelet function, increasing bruising and bleeding risk despite a normal or near-normal platelet count.

### Electrolyte, acid-base and endocrine effects

- **Hyperkalaemia:** Reduced potassium excretion can produce hyperkalaemia, which may cause dangerous cardiac conduction abnormalities and dysrhythmias.
- **Metabolic acidosis:** Reduced bicarbonate regulation and acid excretion lead to metabolic acidosis, contributing to fatigue, muscle wasting and bone effects.
- **Anaemia:** Reduced erythropoietin (EPO) production lowers red-cell production, causing a usually normocytic anaemia with fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance.
- **Mineral changes:** Reduced vitamin D activation can contribute to hypocalcaemia, while reduced phosphate excretion causes hyperphosphataemia.

### Clinical manifestations of CKD

- **Urine and fluid:** Early disease may cause polyuria and nocturia; later disease can cause oliguria, fluid retention, oedema and hypertension.
- **Systemic:** Anaemia, fatigue, pruritus, anorexia, nausea and muscle wasting are common complications of advanced CKD.
- **Neurological:** Uraemia may produce poor concentration, confusion, drowsiness, paraesthesia or seizures in severe cases.
- **Cardiovascular:** Hypertension, dyslipidaemia, vascular calcification and fluid overload increase cardiovascular risk.

### Assessing a person with CKD

- **History:** Ask about diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, renal and urinary symptoms, medicines, nephrotoxins, family history and previous AKI.
- **Laboratory tests:** Review eGFR and creatinine, urea, electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, full blood count, glucose and relevant urine studies.
- **Urinalysis:** Assess protein, blood, specific gravity and other abnormalities; quantify proteinuria or albuminuria when ordered.
- **Bedside assessment:** Measure blood pressure, daily weight, oedema, urine output, fluid balance, nutritional status and cardiovascular risk factors.

### Three management goals

- **Preserve function:** Identify and treat the cause, avoid nephrotoxins and maintain appropriate fluid and medication management.
- **Slow progression:** Control blood pressure and diabetes, reduce proteinuria when appropriate and support nutrition and lifestyle changes.
- **Reduce cardiovascular risk:** Manage dyslipidaemia, smoking, weight, activity, blood pressure and other modifiable cardiovascular risks.
- **Shared planning:** Education and shared decision-making should begin early, including discussion of renal replacement options if progression is likely.

### CKD management strategies

- **Fluid balance:** Individualise fluid and sodium intake, monitor weight and oedema, and prescribe diuretics or fluid restriction when indicated.
- **Blood pressure:** ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) may reduce blood pressure and proteinuria; monitor renal function and potassium.
- **Nutrition:** Coordinate renal nutrition advice for sodium, potassium, phosphate, protein and energy according to stage and treatment.
- **Anaemia and diabetes:** Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), iron assessment and diabetes management may be required.
- **Education:** Teach medicine safety, sick-day advice where applicable, symptom monitoring, follow-up and when to seek urgent help.

### Hyperkalaemia is an emergency

- **Why it matters:** High potassium can alter cardiac conduction and cause life-threatening dysrhythmias, sometimes with few symptoms.
- **Assessment:** Repeat or confirm an unexpected result as clinically appropriate, assess the patient, obtain an ECG and commence cardiac monitoring when indicated.
- **Escalation:** Notify the treating team urgently and follow the local emergency protocol; treatment may stabilise the myocardium, shift potassium into cells and remove potassium from the body.
- **Nursing care:** Review medicines and sources of potassium, maintain IV access if required, monitor ECG, repeat blood tests and document response.

### CKD mineral and bone disorder

- **Mechanism:** Phosphate retention and reduced vitamin D activation lower available calcium and stimulate parathyroid hormone release.
- **Secondary hyperparathyroidism:** Persistently increased parathyroid hormone increases bone turnover and attempts to restore serum calcium.
- **High-turnover disease:** Excessive PTH activity can cause high-turnover bone disease, bone pain, weakness and fracture risk.
- **Low-turnover disease:** Oversuppression of PTH or other factors can cause low-turnover or adynamic bone disease, in which bone remodelling is reduced.
- **Management:** Monitor calcium, phosphate, PTH and vitamin D; use prescribed dietary measures, phosphate binders and vitamin D therapies as appropriate.

### Principles of dialysis

- **Diffusion:** Solutes move across a semi-permeable membrane from an area of higher concentration to lower concentration, down a concentration gradient.
- **Osmosis:** Water moves across a semi-permeable membrane towards the solution with the higher effective solute concentration.
- **Ultrafiltration:** Fluid is removed across a membrane by a pressure gradient; the prescribed fluid removal is the ultrafiltration goal.
- **Purpose:** Dialysis replaces selected kidney functions by removing waste and excess fluid and helping correct electrolyte and acid-base disturbances.

### Haemodialysis and vascular access

- **Dialyser:** Blood flows through fibres in a dialyser while dialysis fluid flows on the other side of a semi-permeable membrane; waste and excess fluid are removed.
- **Access:** An arteriovenous (AV) fistula joins an artery to a vein; an AV graft uses a synthetic conduit. A central venous catheter may be used when necessary.
- **Fistula assessment:** Check for a bruit and thrill as directed, inspect for infection or bleeding, and protect the access arm from blood pressure measurements, venepuncture and constriction.
- **Process:** Two needles or access limbs allow blood to leave the patient, pass through the dialyser and return after treatment; anticoagulation is often used in the circuit.

### Haemodialysis nursing care

- **Before treatment:** Record weight and observations, assess fluid status, review blood results and medicines, inspect access and confirm the prescribed dialysis plan.
- **During treatment:** Monitor blood pressure, symptoms, access, machine alarms, ultrafiltration and complications such as cramps, headache, nausea or hypotension.
- **After treatment:** Recheck weight and observations, assess for bleeding and post-dialysis symptoms, and reinforce access care and fluid or diet advice.
- **Safety:** Do not use the fistula or graft arm for routine blood pressure measurement or venepuncture unless specifically directed by local policy.

### Peritoneal dialysis

- **Membrane and catheter:** The peritoneum acts as the semi-permeable membrane; a catheter delivers dialysis fluid into and out of the peritoneal cavity.
- **Dwell phase:** Dialysis fluid remains in the abdomen for the prescribed dwell time while diffusion and ultrafiltration occur.
- **Exchange:** Used fluid is drained and replaced with fresh solution using aseptic technique; automated or continuous ambulatory regimens may be used.
- **Risks:** Peritonitis, catheter infection, leaks, hernia, inadequate drainage and fluid or glucose-related problems require prompt assessment.
- **Warning signs:** Cloudy effluent, abdominal pain, fever, nausea or malaise may indicate peritonitis and require urgent escalation.

### Kidney transplantation

- **Placement:** A donor kidney is usually placed in the pelvis, where its vessels and ureter can be connected to the recipient's circulation and bladder.
- **Immunosuppression:** Lifelong immunosuppressive medicines reduce rejection risk but increase susceptibility to infection and some malignancies.
- **Nursing priorities:** Monitor urine output, renal function, fluid balance, wound, infection, blood pressure and medicine effects; teach strict adherence and follow-up.
- **Rejection or infection:** Reduced urine output, rising creatinine, fever, graft pain or other changes require prompt clinical review rather than self-management.

### Education and self-management

- **Daily monitoring:** Teach the patient to notice changing weight, oedema, breathlessness, urine output and blood pressure when home monitoring is prescribed.
- **Medicine safety:** Review doses and timing, avoid non-prescribed nephrotoxins such as NSAIDs unless specifically directed, and check before using over-the-counter products.
- **Access protection:** Keep dialysis access clean, check for infection or loss of bruit/thrill, and seek urgent help for bleeding or access failure.
- **Continuity:** Coordinate renal, dietetic, pharmacy and social support; ensure the patient knows dialysis appointments, dietary advice and escalation contacts.

**Q1.** CKD is defined as kidney damage or reduced GFR persisting for at least:

- 2 years

**Q2.** Match the renal change with its likely consequence.

- Reduced EPO production -> Anaemia
- Reduced potassium excretion -> Hyperkalaemia
- Reduced bicarbonate regulation -> Metabolic acidosis
- Reduced phosphate excretion -> Hyperphosphataemia

**Q3.** Put the broad CKD progression stages in the correct order.

1. Stage 1: normal or high GFR with kidney damage
2. Stage 2: mildly reduced GFR
3. Stage 3: moderately reduced GFR
4. Stage 4: severely reduced GFR and renal replacement planning
5. Stage 5: kidney failure or end-stage renal failure

**Q4.** A thrill and bruit are useful checks of AV fistula function.

- Answer: **True** - A palpable thrill and audible bruit suggest flow through the fistula; a new absence or change requires prompt assessment.

**Q5.** Which CKD complication requires urgent ECG assessment and escalation?

- Mild pruritus
- **Hyperkalaemia** (correct)
- Stable nocturia
- Chronic dry skin

**Q6.** Match each dialysis principle with its description.

- Diffusion -> Solute movement down a concentration gradient
- Osmosis -> Water movement towards higher effective solute concentration
- Ultrafiltration -> Fluid removal driven by a pressure gradient

**Q7.** Peritoneal dialysis uses the peritoneum as the semi-permeable membrane.

- Answer: **True** - Dialysis fluid is instilled into the peritoneal cavity and the patient's peritoneum acts as the exchange membrane.

**Q8.** Which finding is most concerning for peritonitis in a patient receiving peritoneal dialysis?

- Clear effluent with no symptoms
- **Cloudy effluent with abdominal pain and fever** (correct)
- A stable appetite
- A functioning catheter with normal drainage

**Q9.** Put the main haemodialysis sequence in the correct order.

1. Assess the patient, weight, observations and vascular access
2. Blood leaves through the dialysis access
3. Blood passes through the dialyser as waste and fluid are removed
4. Processed blood returns to the patient
5. Reassess weight, observations, access and post-treatment symptoms

**Q10.** The AV fistula arm should routinely be used for blood pressure measurement to save the other arm.

- Answer: **False** - Protect the fistula or graft arm: avoid routine blood pressure measurement, venepuncture and constriction unless local policy specifically directs otherwise.
