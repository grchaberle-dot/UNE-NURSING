\u2728 What is Critical Thinking?

Critical thinking is a complex process of deliberation involving a wide range of skills and attitudes (Cottrell, 2017). It includes:

  • Identifying other people's positions, arguments, and conclusions
  • Evaluating evidence for alternative points of view
  • Weighing up opposing arguments fairly
  • Reading between the lines and identifying false assumptions
  • Recognising persuasive techniques (false logic, emotional appeal)
  • Synthesising information to form your own position
  • Presenting a point of view in a structured, well-reasoned way
Critical thinking is a METHOD, not a personality trait. Both sceptical and trusting people need structured approaches. It's about evidence, not gut feeling.

\uD83E\uDD14 Scepticism, Trust & Reasoning

Scepticism

Scepticism in critical thinking means "polite doubt": holding open the possibility that what you know may be only part of the picture (Ennis, 1987). It doesn't mean being cynical or never believing anything.

Reasoning

  • Having reasons for what we believe and being aware of them
  • Critically evaluating our own beliefs and actions
  • Presenting reasons to others in a structured way

Critical Analysis of Others' Reasoning

  • Identifying their reasons and conclusions
  • Analysing how they select and combine reasons
  • Evaluating whether reasons support conclusions
  • Checking if reasons are well-founded and evidence-based
  • Identifying flaws in reasoning
Start with your own assumptions first. Questioning your own reasoning is the hardest and most important part.

\u26A0\uFE0F Barriers to Critical Thinking

Lack of Methods/Strategies

Not knowing which steps to take to improve thinking skills. Strategies from school may not be rigorous enough for university-level work.

Lack of Practice

Like any skill, critical thinking requires regular practice. Most people can develop it with effort (Cottrell, 2017).

Reluctance to Critique Experts

Feeling it's "alien" or "rude" to question published work. But critical analysis IS what teaching expects at university.

Affective (Emotional) Reasons

Evidence that challenges deeply held beliefs can cause anxiety or frustration, blocking clear thinking. Emotional self-management is essential.

Mistaking Info for Understanding

Preferring facts and "right answers" over developing insight. Students who say "just give me the answer" are showing this barrier (Cowell et al., 1995).

Cultural Barriers

Some cultures emphasise repeating expert words over questioning them. University expects both respect AND critical analysis.

\uD83D\uDCAA Underlying Skills

  • Attention to detail: Note small clues that shed light on the overall issue
  • Identifying trends and patterns: Careful mapping and analysis of data
  • Repetition: Go over the same ground several times to catch missed points
  • Taking different perspectives: Look at the same information from multiple angles
  • Objectivity: Set aside personal likes/beliefs to gain deeper understanding
  • Considering implications: What seems good short-term may have consequences later

\uD83D\uDCAD Self-Awareness

Self-awareness requires courage: questioning your own assumptions, preconceptions, biases, and beliefs. Good critical thinkers reflect on their:

  • Motivations and personal agendas
  • Prejudices and preconceived ideas
  • Areas of expertise vs gaps in knowledge
  • Emotional triggers and responses
  • The evidence they use to support their own beliefs
"Both positives and negatives": In academic contexts, criticism = analysis of strengths AND weaknesses. It's about what works and what doesn't, and WHY.
