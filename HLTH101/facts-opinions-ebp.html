\u2728 Facts vs Opinions

Facts

Objective, verifiable statements supported by evidence. Can be tested and confirmed. Treatment decisions must be based on facts.

Opinions

Subjective views influenced by personal experience, beliefs, and bias. "I've always done it this way" is an opinion, not evidence.

A colleague's habit is an opinion. A systematic review showing Treatment A has better outcomes is evidence. Know the difference.

\uD83D\uDD2C Evidence-Based Practice (EBP)

Three Pillars of EBP

EBP combines (Hew & Cheung, 2014):

  1. Best research evidence: Hard scientific evidence from quality studies
  2. Clinical expertise: The practitioner's skill and experience
  3. Patient values and preferences: What matters to the individual patient
EBP improves patient outcomes, reduces errors, and lowers healthcare costs. It's the standard of professional nursing practice.

\uD83D\uDCDA Levels of Evidence

\uD83E\uDD47 Primary

Original research studies. Strongest level of evidence. Direct investigation and data collection.

\uD83E\uDD48 Secondary

Authorised evaluation or review of original research. Literature reviews, meta-analyses.

\uD83E\uDD49 Tertiary

Textbooks, summaries, encyclopedias. Most common but weakest level of evidence.

\u2705 Validity & Reliability

Validity

Is the information accurate and correct? Does the experiment measure what it claims to? (Hiltbrand, 2019)

4 types: content, face, criterion, construct (Middleton, 2019)

Nursing example: Are care plans and medications valid for the diagnosis?

Reliability

Is the information trustworthy and repeatable? Can results be reproduced consistently? (Shuttleworth, 2019)

Nursing example: Does the BP cuff give the same reading each time?

Reliability provides trust, quality, and safety (Kemper & Boyle, 2009)

A BP cuff giving different readings each time = not reliable. A treatment tested on knee pain but applied to headaches = not valid. Both must be present.

\uD83D\uDD0D Evaluating Sources (CRAAP Test)

CriterionAsk Yourself
CurrencyIs it recent enough? When was it published/updated?
RelevanceDoes it relate to your topic?
AuthorityWho wrote it? What are their qualifications? Reputable institution?
AccuracyIs there a reference list? Is it peer-reviewed? Can info be verified?
PurposeWhy was it written? To inform, persuade, sell, entertain? Any bias?
A peer-reviewed article in the Medical Journal of Australia beats a blog post by someone with no credentials. Always check who, why, and whether it's referenced.
