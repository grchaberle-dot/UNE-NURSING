# Chronic Illness and Nursing Care

**Source:** Southern Cross University, Formation Based Learning: Chronic Illness and Nursing Care (Studocu). Australian.

---

## Definition

A chronic disease is an impairment or health condition that lasts longer than **six months**. Chronic diseases usually develop over a long period, follow a prolonged course, and **cannot be directly transmitted** from one person to another. They may result from interactions among **genetics, lifestyle and environmental factors**.

Chronic illness can produce a substantial burden for the individual, family, community, health-care system, workforce and government. Some chronic diseases contribute mainly to premature mortality, whereas others contribute more significantly to disability and reduced quality of life.

---

## Six Common Characteristics

- **Complexity:** multiple causes, interacting risk factors, comorbidities, long-term treatment requirements
- **Long development period:** may develop gradually before symptoms become clinically apparent
- **Prolonged course:** care may continue for many years or throughout life
- **Functional impairment or disability:** may restrict mobility, work, education, social participation, self-care or family roles
- **Need for ongoing management:** monitoring, medication, lifestyle changes, education, follow-up, coordination among services
- **Potential for complications:** may cause additional disease, disability, hospitalisation and reduced life expectancy

---

## Examples of Chronic Conditions

Ischaemic heart disease (coronary heart disease), stroke, lung cancer, colorectal cancer, depression, type 2 diabetes, arthritis, osteoporosis, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, oral disease.

### National Health Priority Areas

- Asthma
- Arthritis
- Cardiovascular disease
- Diabetes
- Cancer

## Burden and Increasing Importance

Chronic disease is one of the major health-care challenges because more people require long-term treatment, disability support, community services, rehabilitation and assistance with daily living.

The increasing burden places pressure on:

- Acute hospitals and emergency departments
- Health-care expenditure
- The workforce and productivity
- Families and informal carers
- Community support services
- Routine activities and social participation
- Quality of life

**The burden is not distributed equally.** Disadvantaged populations experience higher rates of chronic illness, partly because of **social determinants of health**.

---

## Modifiable Risk Factors

- Poor diet
- Physical inactivity
- Obesity
- Tobacco smoking
- Risky alcohol consumption
- Social and socioeconomic disadvantage
- Environmental influences

Genetic predisposition and other non-modifiable factors may also contribute.

> Nurses should consider how social circumstances influence exposure to risk factors, access to care, treatment adherence, health literacy and the ability to make lifestyle changes. Telling someone to eat better ignores whether they can afford to.

---

## Comorbidity

**Comorbidity** means the presence of **two or more health conditions** in the same person. Comorbidity increases the complexity of assessment, treatment, medication management, discharge planning and self-care.

### The key Australian triad

There is an important association among **cardiovascular disease, diabetes and chronic kidney disease**. The presence of these conditions increases with age.

More than **one in four Australian adults** had cardiovascular disease, diabetes or chronic kidney disease, with approximately **1.2 million people** experiencing at least two of these conditions and some experiencing all three.

### Consequences of comorbidity

- More complex medication regimens
- Increased risk of medication interactions and adverse effects
- More frequent hospitalisation
- Greater functional impairment
- Difficulty following multiple treatment plans
- Increased need for coordinated and multidisciplinary care

> Find one of the triad and actively screen for the other two.

## Acute vs Chronic Models of Care

### Acute-care model

Designed for short-term, urgent problems. Commonly focuses on diagnosis, rapid treatment, stabilisation and discharge. **This model alone is not sufficient** to meet projected health-care needs, because chronic illness requires continuing, coordinated, preventive and person-centred care.

### Chronic-care model

Optimal chronic care requires a planned, collaborative approach that supports the person across health-care settings and over time.

**Five key principles:**

1. Provide **holistic** care
2. Adopt a **patient-centred** approach
3. Provide care that is **coordinated and continuous**
4. Recognise that chronic illness affects **all dimensions of personhood**
5. Recognise and respect **cultural responses** to illness

**Holistic care** considers physical, psychological, social, cultural, spiritual, financial, educational and occupational effects.

**Patient-centred care** treats the person as an **active partner** rather than a passive recipient of treatment.

---

## National Chronic Disease Strategy

Adopted in **2005** to support the management of chronic disease. Its major directions:

1. **Prevent** disease, disease progression and associated comorbidities
2. **Detect and treat** disease early to improve health outcomes
3. **Integrate** services and coordinate care among providers
4. **Enable self-management** so patients can care for themselves and achieve the best possible outcomes

### Framework approach

The framework aims to reduce the impact of chronic disease and improve outcomes for individuals, families, communities and society. The approach includes:

- Improving services for specific diseases
- Using cost-effective interventions
- Focusing on consumers and their needs
- Prioritising populations that are disproportionately affected

## Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Considerations

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are more likely than other Australians to experience poor health, disability and premature death. **Socioeconomic disadvantage is a major health risk** and was identified as accounting for a substantial proportion of the life-expectancy gap in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in the Northern Territory.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations experience substantially higher rates of some chronic diseases. **Diabetes rates are approximately four times higher** than in other Australians.

This difference must be understood in relation to **social determinants of health**, including access, affordability, housing, education, employment, food security, transport and culturally safe services.

### Eight essential elements of chronic care

1. **Identification:** identify people at risk and those who already have chronic disease
2. **Trust:** establish culturally safe, respectful and reliable relationships
3. **Screening and assessment:** detect disease and assess the person's individual needs
4. **Clinical indicators:** monitor diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease and lung disease
5. **Timely treatment:** initiate appropriate treatment without avoidable delay
6. **Education:** improve health literacy using accessible and culturally appropriate communication
7. **Referral:** consider affordability, access, availability, consultation and timeliness
8. **Follow-up:** use a local and continuing approach rather than relying only on episodic hospital care

Nurses should avoid assumptions, involve the person and family appropriately, use interpreters when required, support culturally safe communication, and collaborate with Aboriginal health services and other relevant community services.

> After identification comes trust. Without that, nothing else in the model works.

## The Nurse's Role in Chronic Illness

Nurses have a central role in preventing, detecting, managing and coordinating chronic disease care.

- Acting as an **entry point** to health services
- Identifying risks and early signs of chronic disease
- Preventing or delaying disease onset and progression
- Providing health information and education
- Promoting health literacy and self-management
- Placing the patient at the centre of care
- Advocating for patient needs and equitable access
- Identifying and facilitating the use of health resources
- Empowering communities to improve health and wellbeing
- Coordinating care and communicating with other providers
- Collaborating with other sectors and disciplines
- Contributing to health policy advocacy
- Providing cost-effective, evidence-informed care
- Monitoring treatment response and preventing complications

**The nurse should assess the patient's physical condition as well as the effects of illness on family roles, work, education, finances, relationships, mental health, culture and daily routines.**

## Lived Experience of Chronic Illness

Chronic illness affects almost every area of a person's life. A person may feel like a **burden** to family, friends, health professionals, employers or lecturers. Pain and fatigue may make ordinary activities difficult and may lead to missed social, academic and employment opportunities.

### Common effects

- Reduced ability to function during periods of severe pain
- Social isolation and loneliness
- Loss of work hours and financial stress
- Difficulty maintaining employment or communicating with employers
- Disruption to education and academic opportunities
- Difficulty adapting to diet, exercise and stress-management requirements
- Time-consuming and painful appointments, medications and procedures
- Need to incorporate regular surgery, hormonal therapy or alternative therapy into daily life
- Emotional effects associated with uncertainty, symptoms and dependence on others

### Support needs

Support may be required from family, friends, employers, universities, student services, general practitioners, specialists, psychologists and other members of the health-care team.

**Nursing support should include:**

- Open communication
- **Validation** of the person's experience
- Realistic goal setting
- Education
- Referral to appropriate services
- Advocacy for reasonable accommodations

**Care planning should recognise that adjustment is ongoing** and that the person may need different support at different stages of illness.

> Validation is a genuine intervention, not just being nice. Being believed changes how people engage with treatment.

## High-Yield Examination Checklist

- Define chronic illness as a condition lasting longer than six months
- Remember the characteristic pattern: complex, gradual development, prolonged course, possible disability
- Discuss the burden on patients, families, communities, health services, workforce and economy
- Identify modifiable risks: poor diet, inactivity, obesity, smoking, risky alcohol consumption
- Explain how comorbidity increases care complexity and risk
- Use holistic, patient-centred, coordinated, continuous, culturally safe care
- Recognise the importance of trust, access, screening, timely treatment, education, referral and follow-up for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples
- Differentiate asthma as generally variable and reversible from COPD as progressive and usually irreversible
- Recognise a silent chest, confusion, drowsiness, exhaustion, inability to speak and persistent hypoxaemia as emergency findings
- Interpret low pH, raised PaCO2, low PaO2 and normal HCO3 as acute respiratory acidosis with hypoxaemia
- For IV therapy, verify patient, order, medication or fluid, dose or volume, route, rate, expiry, allergies, compatibility, equipment and documentation
- Use aseptic technique, assess cannula patency, never force a flush, obtain an independent double-check, monitor continuously after commencement
