# Aboriginal Health: History, Worldviews & Cultural Safety

**Unit:** OORA400  
**Module ID:** `aboriginalHealthDeep`

> Trace colonisation as a continuing health determinant, understand holistic Aboriginal health, the Stolen Generations' legacy, Closing the Gap and culturally safe nursing.

---

## Learn

### Pre-colonial health and wellbeing

- **Nations:** Before colonisation, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples belonged to more than 500 distinct nations, with their own languages, law, kinship systems and cultural practices.
- **Wellbeing:** Health was supported by connected food systems, movement, ceremony, relationships, responsibility to Country and social structures, not only by treating illness.

### Food systems, law and social structures

- **Knowledge:** Seasonal ecological knowledge guided harvesting, hunting, fishing and caring for resources. Knowledge was held and passed on through family, story, practice and ceremony.
- **Order:** Lore and kinship organised obligations, authority, care, marriage, education and relationships between people, animals, land and the spiritual world.

### Invasion and colonisation

- **Timeline:** James Cook charted the east coast in 1770; the First Fleet arrived in 1788. The doctrine of terra nullius denied Aboriginal sovereignty and treated Country as belonging to no one.
- **Health impact:** Frontier warfare, dispossession and introduced diseases disrupted communities, food systems, mobility, cultural practice and access to Country.

### Protection and institutional control

- **Control:** Protection policies established reserves and missions and regulated movement, employment, residence, marriage and contact with family and Country.
- **Dependency:** Rations and institutional rules could replace self-determined food systems and livelihoods. These systems should be understood as coercive control, not simply welfare.

### The Stolen Generations

- **Removal:** Aboriginal children were forcibly removed from families from 1814 onwards. NSW legislation in 1909 strengthened state control, and a national policy in 1937 promoted removal and assimilation.
- **Experiences:** Many children experienced separation, loss of language and identity, institutional neglect, racism, abuse and disrupted family connections after removal.

### Legacy of forced removal

- **Report:** The Bringing Them Home report documented the effects of forced removal and made recommendations for repair, recognition and support.
- **Recognition:** The 2008 National Apology acknowledged the pain and loss caused by the Stolen Generations. Acknowledgement does not by itself resolve ongoing inequity or trauma.

### Colonisation as an ongoing determinant

- **Continuity:** Colonisation is not only a past event: loss of Country, cultural suppression, racism, institutional mistrust and intergenerational trauma continue to shape health and service access.
- **Nursing implication:** A culturally safe nurse connects present health experiences with these determinants without reducing a person or community to disadvantage.

### Country and connection

- **More than geography:** Country includes land, waters, skies, ancestors, living things, stories, law, family, identity and spirituality. It is a relationship and responsibility, not merely a location.
- **Clinical relevance:** Being away from Country, or being unable to practise culture and maintain family connections, may affect wellbeing, recovery and willingness to engage with services.

### A holistic Aboriginal health model

- **Whole person:** Holistic health includes physical, mental, emotional, social, cultural and spiritual dimensions, together with family, community and Country.
- **Wider determinants:** Political and economic conditions and the environment also shape health. Assessment should therefore explore strengths, relationships, safety, culture and context, not just symptoms.

### Constructing the health gap

- **Patterns:** Cardiovascular disease, cancer and injury are among leading causes of illness and death. These patterns must be interpreted alongside colonisation, poverty, racism, housing, food security, service access and other determinants.
- **Data limits:** Population statistics can hide diversity between nations, communities and individuals. Data may be incomplete or shaped by how Aboriginality is recorded, so avoid treating averages as individual predictions.

### Strengths-based practice

- **Avoid deficit narratives:** Deficit-only accounts can reproduce stereotypes and obscure culture, resilience, leadership, family, community-controlled services, knowledge and survival.
- **Reframe:** Use strengths-based language: recognise structural barriers while identifying the person's goals, capabilities, protective relationships, cultural resources and sources of healing.

### Social justice and human rights

- **Four elements:** A rights-based health service is available, accessible, acceptable and of good quality.
- **Meaning:** Availability requires services and resources; accessibility includes affordability, physical access and non-discrimination; acceptability respects culture and ethics; quality means safe, evidence-informed care.

### Closing the Gap

- **Aims:** Closing the Gap seeks to improve health, wellbeing, life outcomes and self-determination for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples.
- **Partnership:** Effective action requires Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander leadership, genuine partnership, accountability, culturally safe services and attention to structural determinants. Paternalistic solutions can reproduce the problem.

### Culturally safe nursing

- **Self-reflection:** Examine your assumptions, power, privilege and the effects of institutional racism. Cultural safety is determined by the recipient of care, not declared by the nurse.
- **Practice:** Ask rather than assume; use a preferred name; arrange an interpreter when needed; involve family with consent; support connection to Aboriginal health workers; and challenge racism.

### Lorri: a case for safe care

- **Risk:** Lorri, an Aboriginal woman in an emergency department, was assumed to be intoxicated. She was actually hypoglycaemic, and delayed triage and an unsafe handover increased risk.
- **Learning:** Do not let stereotypes replace assessment. Check immediate clinical needs, document objectively, escalate deterioration, communicate a structured handover and address discriminatory assumptions.

### Nurses as agents of change

- **Action:** Nurses can advocate for patients, support Aboriginal community-controlled services, contribute to research and education, influence policy and improve systems.
- **Courage:** Challenging racism includes speaking up about unsafe language or practice, seeking supervision, reporting concerns through appropriate pathways and helping make accountability routine.

## Practice (10 questions)

**Q1.** Which statement best describes Country in a holistic Aboriginal health worldview?

- A geographic area used for housing only
- **A relationship including land, waters, law, family, identity and spirituality** (correct)
- A hospital catchment boundary
- A person's birthplace with no ongoing responsibilities

**Q2.** What is the most culturally safe response when a nurse is unsure how a patient wishes to be addressed?

- Use the name on the admission form without checking
- **Ask the person their preferred name and pronunciation** (correct)
- Ask a nearby patient to decide
- Avoid using any name during the admission

**Q3.** Colonisation can continue to influence health through racism, loss of Country, institutional mistrust and intergenerational trauma.

- Answer: **True** - Colonisation is an ongoing health determinant as well as a historical process; its effects remain present in structures, relationships and lived experience.

**Q4.** Match each human-rights element with its meaning.

- Availability -> Services and resources exist
- Accessibility -> People can reach and use care without discrimination or unreasonable barriers
- Acceptability -> Care respects culture, ethics and people's preferences
- Quality -> Care is safe, appropriate and evidence-informed

**Q5.** Place these events in chronological order.

1. Cook charts the east coast (1770)
2. First Fleet arrives (1788)
3. NSW legislation strengthens child removal (1909)
4. National Apology (2008)

**Q6.** Which approach avoids a deficit-only narrative?

- Describe only disease statistics
- Assume culture is a barrier to treatment
- **Recognise structural barriers alongside strengths, leadership and cultural resources** (correct)
- Avoid asking about culture because it is irrelevant

**Q7.** Cultural safety is achieved whenever a nurse believes they have provided culturally respectful care.

- Answer: **False** - Cultural safety is determined by the person receiving care and requires reflection on power, assumptions and racism; it is not self-certified by the nurse.

**Q8.** Lorri is labelled intoxicated but appears unwell. What should the nurse do first?

- Accept the label and wait for routine review
- **Undertake an immediate clinical assessment, including checking urgent causes such as hypoglycaemia** (correct)
- Discharge her to avoid reinforcing stereotypes
- Ask security to remove her

**Q9.** Match each culturally safe nursing action with its purpose.

- Ask rather than assume -> Learn the person's needs and preferences
- Use an interpreter when needed -> Support accurate, equitable communication
- Involve family with consent -> Respect relationships and shared support
- Challenge racism -> Reduce unsafe structural and interpersonal practice

**Q10.** Population health statistics should be treated as exact predictions of every Aboriginal person's health.

- Answer: **False** - Data can be incomplete and may hide diversity between communities. Statistics guide equity work but must not replace individual assessment or strengths-based care.
