# NP Competencies in Aged Care

**Unit:** HSNS370  
**Module ID:** `npAgedCare`

> Explore nurse practitioner competencies in residential aged care: holistic assessment, diagnostic investigations, evidence-based care planning and family education.

---

## Learn

### The NP role in residential aged care

- **Access:** Nurse practitioners help bridge gaps in timely GP access for residents and provide advanced nursing assessment and management within scope.
- **Deterioration:** They recognise and respond to deterioration, including deciding when urgent medical review or transfer is required.
- **Coordination:** They coordinate multidisciplinary team care, communicate with residents and families, and support continuity across services.

### Competency 1.1: holistic assessment

- **Admission history:** Gather the presenting concern, past history, medicines, allergies, baseline function, goals, risks and relevant advance care information.
- **Whole person:** Include physical, cognitive, mood, functional, social, cultural and spiritual assessment.
- **Partnership:** Ask the resident and chosen family or supporters what matters to them and incorporate their preferences into care.

### Physical and functional assessment

- **Physical:** Assess vital signs, pain, nutrition, hydration, skin, continence, mobility and systems relevant to the presentation.
- **Function:** Establish baseline transfers, gait, activities of daily living, communication and sensory needs.
- **Clinical meaning:** Compare current findings with baseline and identify deterioration, risks and strengths rather than relying on one measurement.

### Cognitive, mood and psychosocial assessment

- **Cognition:** Consider baseline cognition, acute change, delirium risk, decision-making capacity and communication method.
- **Mood:** Explore depression, anxiety, distress, sleep, loneliness and the person's experience of residential care.
- **Context:** Ask about relationships, meaningful activity, culture, identity, spiritual needs and social supports.

### Challenges in older-person assessment

- **Communication:** Memory impairment, hearing loss, language differences or aphasia may make history-taking difficult.
- **Under-reporting:** People may minimise pain or symptoms because of stoicism, fear, cognitive impairment or concern about burdening staff.
- **Response:** Use accessible communication, interpreters, collateral history and observation; allow time and create psychological safety.

### Competency 1.2: diagnostic investigations

- **Examples:** Investigations may include full blood examination (FBE), vital signs, urine testing, wound swabs, ECG, chest X-ray and bladder scanning.
- **Clinical question:** Choose investigations to answer a focused clinical question and interpret results with history and examination.
- **Safety:** Check consent, preparation, allergies, specimen identity, escalation thresholds and follow-up of results.

### SOAP and investigations

- **Subjective:** Record the resident's symptoms, concerns, relevant history and collateral reports.
- **Objective:** Record observations, examination findings, vital signs and available investigation results.
- **Assessment and plan:** State clinical reasoning, differentials, actions, treatment, escalation, monitoring and review arrangements.

### Interpreting investigations safely

- **FBE:** A full blood examination may support assessment of anaemia, infection or platelet-related issues, but results need clinical context.
- **Urine testing:** Interpret urine findings with symptoms and assessment; avoid treating an isolated result as infection without clinical correlation.
- **Other tests:** ECG, chest X-ray, wound swabs and bladder scans are selected for the presentation and require appropriate follow-up.

### Competency 2.1: translate knowledge into care

- **Evidence-based practice:** Integrate best available evidence with clinical expertise, the resident's preferences, context and goals.
- **Adaptation:** Adapt evidence to aged-care resources, comorbidities, cognition, culture, functional ability and risk-benefit balance.
- **Health promotion:** Include prevention, self-management, function, comfort and quality of life, not only disease treatment.

### Implementation and professional development

- **Barriers:** Barriers may include time, staffing, resources, competing priorities, communication gaps, resistance to change and limited access to evidence.
- **Implementation:** Use clear goals, education, teamwork, audit or feedback and review of outcomes to embed a care plan.
- **Development:** Maintain professional development, reflect on practice and update knowledge as evidence and resident needs change.

### Competency 2.2: educate and support others

- **Advance care planning:** Support conversations about values, substitute decision-makers, treatment preferences and end-of-life care at an appropriate pace.
- **Family feedback:** Provide clear, respectful updates, invite questions and confirm understanding while protecting the resident's privacy and autonomy.
- **Team support:** Mentor new nurses, share clinical reasoning and use debriefing after difficult events or significant deterioration.

### Motivation theories in leadership

- **Maslow:** People have needs that range from physiological and safety needs to belonging, esteem and self-actualisation; nursing leadership considers the whole person.
- **ERG:** Alderfer's ERG theory groups motivation into existence, relatedness and growth, which can be pursued flexibly rather than in a strict sequence.
- **Expectancy and equity:** Expectancy theory links effort, performance and valued outcomes; equity theory highlights perceived fairness in inputs, rewards and treatment.

## Practice (7 questions)

**Q1.** Which activity best reflects the NP role in residential aged care?

- Managing only paperwork
- **Bridging access gaps, recognising deterioration and coordinating multidisciplinary care** (correct)
- Replacing every member of the multidisciplinary team
- Avoiding family communication

**Q2.** Match each competency focus with an example.

- 1.1 Comprehensive assessment -> Admission history, physical findings, cognition, function, culture and spirituality
- 1.2 Diagnostic investigations -> Selecting and following up FBE, urine testing, ECG or other clinically indicated tests
- 2.1 Knowledge into care -> Adapting evidence to goals, context and available resources
- 2.2 Education and support -> Advance care planning, family education, mentoring and debriefing

**Q3.** When an older resident under-reports pain, what is the best response?

- Assume there is no pain
- **Use accessible communication, observation, collateral history and appropriate pain tools** (correct)
- Ask only a yes/no question once
- Document that the resident is difficult

**Q4.** A diagnostic test should be ordered without a clinical question because more data are always better.

- Answer: **False** - Investigations should be clinically indicated, interpreted with history and examination, and followed up safely.

**Q5.** Put a SOAP-based clinical assessment sequence in the most useful order.

1. Gather subjective history and collateral information
2. Complete objective assessment and indicated investigations
3. Form an assessment with differentials and clinical reasoning
4. Document a plan, escalation, monitoring and review

**Q6.** Which is an example of translating evidence into an individualised care plan?

- Apply a guideline without considering the resident
- **Integrate evidence with goals, comorbidities, culture, function and context** (correct)
- Use the cheapest intervention regardless of safety
- Exclude the resident from decisions to improve efficiency

**Q7.** Equity theory considers how perceived fairness of treatment and rewards can affect motivation.

- Answer: **True** - Equity theory focuses on perceived fairness when people compare their contributions and outcomes with those of others.
