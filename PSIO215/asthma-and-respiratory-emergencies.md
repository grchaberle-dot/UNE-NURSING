## Asthma Pathophysiology

Asthma is characterised by airway inflammation, airway hyperresponsiveness and variable airflow obstruction.

1. Exposure to an allergen or other trigger activates an inflammatory response
2. **Mast cells** degranulate and release histamine and inflammatory cytokines
3. Airway smooth muscle contracts, producing **bronchoconstriction**
4. The airway mucosa becomes swollen because of **oedema**
5. **Mucus** production increases and may obstruct the airway
6. Airway resistance increases and **expiratory airflow** becomes restricted

During a severe attack, **air trapping** and **respiratory muscle fatigue** may develop.

> Three things narrow the airway: muscle spasm, swelling and mucus. That is why you need a bronchodilator AND a steroid.

## Asthma Compared with COPD

| Feature | Asthma | COPD |
|---|---|---|
| Airway problem | Airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, bronchoconstriction, mucosal oedema, mucus hypersecretion | Progressive airway inflammation and destruction with reduced airflow |
| Progression | Airflow obstruction often **variable and reversible** | Airflow limitation generally **progressive and irreversible** |
| Management | Inhaled corticosteroids, bronchodilators, trigger avoidance | Bronchodilators, pulmonary rehabilitation, lifestyle changes |
| Potential impact | Usually episodic, but severe exacerbations can cause respiratory failure | Progressive reduction in respiratory function and possible respiratory failure over time |

### Other respiratory conditions

**Acute:**
- **Pneumonia:** infection causing alveolar inflammation and consolidation. Complications include sepsis and ARDS
- **Pulmonary embolism:** obstruction of the pulmonary vasculature by a thrombus. May cause hypoxaemia and can progress to cardiac arrest

**Chronic:**
- **Cystic fibrosis:** a genetic disorder causing thick mucus production, recurrent respiratory infections and progressive respiratory impairment
- **Interstitial lung disease:** progressive scarring of lung tissue that impairs gas exchange

## Focused Acute Respiratory Assessment

Use a structured primary survey and escalate promptly when the patient is unstable.

- **Airway:** assess patency and ability to speak
- **Breathing:** respiratory rate, depth, effort, accessory-muscle use, chest movement, work of breathing
- **Circulation:** pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, overall circulatory status
- **Vital signs:** heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature
- **Auscultation:** listen for wheeze, diminished breath sounds, crackles, or a **silent chest**
- **Peak expiratory flow:** compare with the patient's usual or predicted baseline
- **History:** triggers, previous severe attacks, usual medications, response to treatment, relevant comorbidities

## THE SILENT CHEST WARNING

**A silent chest may indicate critically reduced air movement rather than improvement.**

A patient who becomes quiet, drowsy, confused or unable to speak may be **deteriorating** despite apparently reduced wheeze.

### Red flags requiring urgent escalation

- Inability to speak in full sentences
- Increasing respiratory rate or work of breathing
- Severe accessory-muscle use
- **Silent chest or absent breath sounds**
- Persistent hypoxaemia despite oxygen therapy
- Confusion, drowsiness or reduced level of consciousness
- Respiratory fatigue or exhaustion
- Rising carbon-dioxide level and respiratory acidosis

> This is the single most important thing on this page. A quiet chest in a severe asthmatic is an emergency, not a reassurance.

## Diagnostic Tests

- **Spirometry with bronchodilator response:** reversible airflow limitation supports asthma
- **Peak expiratory flow variability:** diurnal variation is more characteristic of asthma than COPD
- **Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO):** an elevated result may indicate eosinophilic airway inflammation
- **Chest radiography:** helps identify or exclude pneumonia, pneumothorax and other causes of dyspnoea. Asthma may show hyperinflated lungs and flattened diaphragms due to air trapping

## Acute Management of Severe Asthma

A patient with severe dyspnoea, accessory-muscle use and inability to complete sentences requires immediate assessment and treatment.

1. Call for urgent clinical assistance and begin a structured primary survey
2. Administer oxygen and titrate to the prescribed or locally approved target
3. Administer nebulised **salbutamol** as prescribed
4. Administer nebulised **ipratropium bromide** as prescribed
5. Administer **systemic corticosteroids** such as oral prednisolone or IV hydrocortisone
6. Monitor respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, mental state, heart rate, respiratory rate and response to treatment
7. Consider **IV magnesium sulfate** or IV salbutamol when not responding adequately to initial bronchodilators, per local protocol
8. Prepare for advanced airway management if there are signs of impending respiratory failure

### Why bronchodilators and corticosteroids together

**Bronchodilators** such as salbutamol stimulate **beta-two receptors** and relax airway smooth muscle. This produces bronchodilation and reduces airway resistance, providing relatively rapid symptom relief.

**Corticosteroids** reduce airway inflammation, eosinophilic infiltration, mucosal swelling and mucus hypersecretion. Their effects are not a substitute for immediate bronchodilation, but they prevent continuing inflammation and further exacerbation.

Together these therapies address **both** acute bronchospasm and the underlying inflammatory process.

### Intravenous magnesium sulfate

May be used in severe asthma when the patient fails to respond adequately to bronchodilators. Acts as a **smooth-muscle relaxant by inhibiting calcium uptake** in airway muscle, thereby reducing bronchospasm. Administration requires appropriate prescribing, monitoring and adherence to local policy.

## ABG Interpretation in Severe Asthma

| Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Low pH | Acidaemia |
| Raised PaCO2 | Respiratory acidosis caused by CO2 retention and impaired ventilation |
| Low PaO2 | Hypoxaemia |
| **Normal HCO3** | **Little or no metabolic compensation, supporting an acute process** |

**Overall:** acute respiratory acidosis with hypoxaemia, consistent with acute respiratory failure in the context of severe asthma.

**Action:** urgent escalation, senior review, close monitoring, possible intensive-care admission and intubation.

> Normal bicarbonate is the giveaway that it is acute. If the kidneys had time to compensate, HCO3 would be raised.

## Escalation, NIPPV and Mechanical Ventilation

### Non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation

May be considered when respiratory distress does not improve with bronchodilator therapy, when there is hypercapnic respiratory failure, or when work of breathing is substantially increased.

**Precautions:**
- Ensure appropriate mask fit and patient comfort
- Monitor closely for worsening fatigue, hypoxaemia or reduced consciousness
- Observe for barotrauma including pneumothorax
- Ensure staff and equipment are immediately available for escalation
- **Do not delay intubation** when the patient is deteriorating or cannot protect the airway

### Indications for intubation and mechanical ventilation

- Respiratory arrest
- Severe respiratory acidosis
- Exhaustion or impending respiratory arrest
- Persistent hypoxaemia despite oxygen therapy
- Failure of non-invasive support or inability to tolerate it
- Reduced consciousness or inability to protect the airway

**Risks:** barotrauma and pneumothorax, ventilator-associated pneumonia, haemodynamic instability caused by positive-pressure ventilation.
