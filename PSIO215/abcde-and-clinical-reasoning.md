# ABCDE, Clinical Reasoning and Condition Pathophysiology

**Source:** Queensland University of Technology CSB601 Introduction to Clinical Therapeutics for Health (Studocu). Australian.

---

## Exam Approach: Abnormal Findings to Nursing Problems

1. **Spot abnormalities:** confusion, crackles, fever, chest pain, abnormal oxygen saturation, altered blood glucose, hypotension, reduced consciousness
2. **Match the condition:** crackles and fever may indicate pneumonia. Chest pain and ST elevation may indicate myocardial infarction
3. **Apply the ABCDE framework:** identify immediate threats to life in priority order
4. **Distinguish actual and potential problems:** state what is already occurring and what may develop
5. **Link interventions to pathophysiology:** explain how each intervention addresses the problem

---

## Actual vs Potential Nursing Problems

| Problem type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Actual** | Already present and supported by assessment findings | Impaired gas exchange shown by reduced oxygen saturation. Hyperglycaemia shown by elevated blood glucose. Infection shown by fever and productive cough |
| **Potential** | A complication that may occur because of the patient's current condition | Respiratory failure, sepsis, shock, arrhythmias, electrolyte imbalance, coma |

### Worked examples

- **Pneumonia:** Actual: impaired gas exchange. Potential: respiratory failure and sepsis
- **Myocardial infarction:** Actual: decreased cardiac output. Potential: cardiogenic shock and arrhythmias
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis:** Actual: metabolic acidosis. Potential: electrolyte imbalance and coma
- **Heart failure:** Actual: fluid overload, impaired cardiac output. Potential: pulmonary oedema, circulatory failure
- **Stroke:** Actual: impaired neurological function. Potential: aspiration, airway compromise, further deterioration
- **Sepsis:** Actual: systemic infection with impaired perfusion. Potential: organ dysfunction, septic shock

## ABCDE Assessment Framework

| Area | Assessment focus | Possible abnormal findings and concerns |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Airway** | Whether the airway is patent and protected | Secretions, reduced consciousness or aspiration risk may threaten patency |
| **B: Breathing** | Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, work of breathing, breath sounds, cough | Increased RR, reduced SpO2, crackles, wheeze or productive cough may indicate impaired gas exchange |
| **C: Circulation** | Heart rate, blood pressure, peripheral perfusion, chest pain, cardiac rhythm | Tachycardia, borderline or low BP, chest pain and ECG changes may indicate reduced perfusion, shock or myocardial injury |
| **D: Disability** | Level of consciousness, neurological status, weakness, speech, **blood glucose** | Confusion, weakness, slurred speech, seizures or reduced consciousness may indicate stroke, hypoglycaemia, severe hyperglycaemia or neurological deterioration |
| **E: Exposure** | Temperature, skin, hydration, oedema, visible signs | Fever may indicate infection. Dry flushed skin may indicate dehydration. Oedema may indicate fluid overload |

In an exam answer, write the assessment in **priority order**. For example: the airway is currently patent but at risk because of increased secretions. Breathing is compromised by an increased respiratory rate, reduced oxygen saturation, crackles and productive cough. Circulation shows tachycardia and borderline blood pressure, suggesting possible early sepsis. Disability and exposure should then be assessed for confusion, blood glucose abnormalities and fever.

> Always check a blood glucose at D. Both hypoglycaemia and HHS can mimic stroke.

## Clinical Reasoning Cycle

1. Consider the patient situation
2. Collect cues
3. Process information
4. Identify problems
5. Establish goals
6. Take action
7. Evaluate outcomes
8. Reflect on the process

Each intervention should be connected to a goal. Positioning a patient upright supports lung expansion. Monitoring oxygen saturation evaluates whether gas exchange is improving.

## Pharmacology: Exam Format

Always state the **drug**, its **action** and **why the patient needs it**.

| Drug or treatment | Action | Reason for use |
|---|---|---|
| Aspirin | Antiplatelet, prevents clot extension | Myocardial infarction: reduces further coronary obstruction |
| Insulin | Reduces blood glucose, suppresses effects of insulin deficiency | Diabetic ketoacidosis and blood glucose management |
| Antibiotics | Kill or control bacteria | Bacterial infection including pneumonia and sepsis |
| Glyceryl trinitrate | Vasodilation, improves cardiac blood flow | Relieves cardiac chest pain, reduces cardiac workload |
| Bronchodilators (salbutamol) | Open narrowed airways | COPD exacerbation, to improve ventilation |
| Diuretics | Remove excess fluid | Heart failure and pulmonary oedema |
| Thrombolytics | Dissolve a clot | Per stroke protocol when an ischaemic stroke is identified |
| Glucose | Restores blood glucose | Given rapidly for hypoglycaemia |

## Condition-Based Pathophysiology and Nursing Priorities

### Pneumonia

**Pathophysiology:** infection causes inflammation of the alveoli. Fluid and pus accumulate, preventing effective oxygen diffusion and causing impaired gas exchange. In a patient with COPD, airway narrowing and mucus further worsen ventilation.

**Signs:** fever, crackles, productive cough, increased respiratory rate, reduced oxygen saturation, possible tachycardia and borderline blood pressure if systemic infection develops.

**Actual:** impaired gas exchange and infection. **Potential:** respiratory failure and sepsis.

**Independent interventions:** position upright to improve lung expansion. Monitor RR, SpO2, breath sounds, temperature, HR and BP. Encourage coughing and deep breathing to clear secretions. Observe for deterioration, confusion or increasing work of breathing.

**Collaborative:** oxygen therapy, antibiotics, prescribed bronchodilators. Escalate if oxygenation or circulation worsens.

### COPD exacerbation

**Pathophysiology:** chronic airway inflammation causes airway narrowing, mucus production and impaired ventilation. During an exacerbation these worsen, causing reduced oxygenation and possible CO2 retention.

**Actual:** impaired ventilation. **Potential:** respiratory failure, worsening CO2 retention.

**Priorities:** position to support breathing, monitor RR and SpO2, assess breath sounds and ABGs, encourage effective breathing techniques, administer controlled oxygen as prescribed, give bronchodilators such as salbutamol. Escalate if respiratory effort, oxygenation or mental status deteriorates.

### Myocardial infarction

**Pathophysiology:** a coronary artery becomes blocked, reducing oxygen and blood flow to the heart muscle. Affected cells become ischaemic and may die, reducing cardiac function.

**Signs:** chest pain, ECG changes such as ST elevation, reduced cardiac output, possible arrhythmias.

**Actual:** decreased cardiac output. **Potential:** cardiogenic shock, arrhythmias, myocardial death.

**Priorities:** assess ABC, monitor ECG, HR, BP and chest pain, provide oxygen if indicated, establish IV access, administer prescribed aspirin, GTN and morphine, provide rest, urgently escalate deterioration.

### Heart failure

**Pathophysiology:** a weak heart pump cannot maintain effective circulation. Blood backs up, causing fluid accumulation in the lungs and peripheral tissues.

**Signs:** crackles, oedema, fluid overload, pulmonary oedema.

**Actual:** fluid overload and impaired cardiac output. **Potential:** pulmonary oedema, worsening circulatory failure.

**Priorities:** position appropriately, monitor respiratory status and SpO2, assess oedema and fluid balance, restrict fluids when prescribed, record daily weights, elevate legs where appropriate, administer prescribed diuretics.

### Stroke

**Pathophysiology:** a sudden reduction in oxygen supply to brain tissue caused by either ischaemia from vessel occlusion or haemorrhage into the brain. Causes brain cell death and neurological impairment.

**Signs:** sudden weakness, slurred speech, altered neurological function, reduced consciousness, possible difficulty protecting the airway.

**Actual:** impaired neurological function. **Potential:** aspiration, airway compromise, further deterioration, death.

**Priorities:** ABCDE assessment, protect the airway, monitor neurological observations, maintain safety, follow the stroke protocol, support urgent assessment for thrombolytic treatment when appropriate for ischaemic stroke.

### Diabetic ketoacidosis

**Pathophysiology:** a profound deficiency of insulin prevents glucose being used effectively by cells. The body breaks down fat for energy, producing acidic **ketones** which cause **metabolic acidosis**. Insulin deficiency also stimulates glucose production in the liver, worsening hyperglycaemia. High blood glucose causes **osmotic diuresis**, producing severe fluid and electrolyte loss (sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, phosphate). Progressive hypovolaemia may lead to shock.

**Signs:** hyperglycaemia, deep rapid **Kussmaul respirations** and dyspnoea, **fruity acetone-smelling breath**, flushed dry skin from dehydration, vomiting, confusion, possible reduced consciousness.

**Actual:** metabolic acidosis, dehydration, hyperglycaemia. **Potential:** electrolyte imbalance, hypovolaemia, shock, arrhythmias, coma.

**Priorities:** frequent ABCDE assessment, monitor blood glucose, neurological status, vital signs, hydration and cardiac rhythm. Administer IV fluids and insulin as prescribed. Monitor electrolytes. Respond promptly to worsening consciousness, perfusion or breathing.

### Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic syndrome (HHS)

**Pathophysiology:** occurs when enough insulin is produced to limit significant ketosis but not enough to prevent severe hyperglycaemia. Hyperglycaemia causes osmotic diuresis and extracellular fluid depletion. Blood glucose may rise substantially before recognition because early symptoms can be less obvious. Increased serum osmolality produces severe neurological manifestations.

**Common associated conditions:** urinary tract infection, pneumonia, sepsis, another acute illness, newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes. Inadequate fluid intake and impaired thirst worsen dehydration.

**Signs:** very high blood glucose, markedly increased serum osmolality, weakness, polyuria, dehydration, somnolence, seizures, coma, hemiparesis or aphasia. **Ketones absent or minimal.**

**Actual:** severe hyperglycaemia, dehydration, altered neurological status. **Potential:** severe neurological deterioration, seizures, coma, hypovolaemia, shock.

**Priorities:** obtain an **immediate blood glucose level**, perform ABCDE, assess neurological status and hydration, monitor vital signs and fluid balance, administer prescribed fluid and insulin therapy.

> HHS can produce stroke-like signs, so rapid blood glucose assessment is essential for correct diagnosis.

### Type 2 diabetes

**Pathophysiology:** insulin is present, but body cells respond poorly to it. This insulin resistance causes persistent hyperglycaemia, which progressively damages blood vessels, nerves and the kidneys.

**Actual:** chronic hyperglycaemia. **Potential:** cardiovascular disease, kidney damage, neuropathy, retinopathy, HHS.

**Priorities:** monitor blood glucose, support diet and exercise education, administer prescribed medication such as metformin, educate about long-term glucose control and complication prevention.

### Hypoglycaemia

**Pathophysiology:** blood glucose falls too low, depriving the brain of its energy supply. Causes neurological and behavioural changes and may progress to seizures or coma.

**Signs:** weakness, trembling, sweating, light-headedness, poor concentration, behavioural change, dizziness, irritability, numbness around the mouth, hunger. Confusion, reduced consciousness, seizures and coma may occur.

**Actual:** altered level of consciousness or neurological function. **Potential:** seizures and coma.

**Priorities:** check the blood glucose level, provide fast-acting glucose according to the **rule of fifteen** when appropriate, recheck the level, monitor consciousness, protect the airway. Escalate if the patient cannot safely swallow or does not improve.

### Obesity

**Pathophysiology:** excess adipose tissue releases inflammatory markers and contributes to insulin resistance. Increases metabolic demand and is associated with diabetes, hypertension and heart disease.

**Actual:** excess adipose tissue, reduced activity tolerance or fatigue where present. **Potential:** insulin resistance, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, metabolic complications.

**Priorities:** lifestyle support, appropriate diet and exercise education, monitor weight and blood glucose, administer prescribed treatment such as orlistat when indicated.

### Sepsis

**Pathophysiology:** a systemic infection causes widespread inflammation and poor tissue perfusion. If it progresses, organ dysfunction and septic shock may develop.

**Signs:** fever, tachycardia, borderline or low blood pressure, altered mental status, evidence of an underlying infection. Pneumonia may present with crackles, productive cough and impaired oxygenation.

**Actual:** systemic infection with impaired tissue perfusion. **Potential:** organ dysfunction, septic shock.

**Priorities:** repeated ABCDE assessments, monitor vital signs, oxygenation, urine output, mental status and perfusion. Administer IV fluids and antibiotics as prescribed. Identify and treat the infection. Escalate promptly if shock or organ dysfunction develops.

## Diabetes Complications

### Macrovascular

Affects large and medium-sized blood vessels. Includes cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease and peripheral vascular disease.

### Microvascular

Involves thickening and damage to vessel membranes in capillaries and arterioles. Usually develops over many years after diagnosis.

- **Diabetic retinopathy:** microvascular damage to the retina can cause blindness. Regular dilated eye examinations are required
- **Diabetic nephropathy:** damage to the small blood vessels supplying the kidney glomeruli can impair kidney function
- **Diabetic neuropathy:** metabolic changes damage nerves, reduce nerve conduction and cause demyelination. Loss of sensation, abnormal sensations, pain and paraesthesia, especially in the lower extremities. **Loss of protective sensation increases the risk of injury and amputation**
- **Dermopathy:** skin changes

## High-Yield Pathophysiology Links

- **Pneumonia:** inflammation and fluid accumulation in the alveoli impair oxygen exchange
- **COPD exacerbation:** airway narrowing and mucus reduce ventilation and worsen oxygenation
- **Myocardial infarction:** coronary artery blockage reduces perfusion, causing myocardial ischaemia and tissue death
- **Heart failure:** a weak cardiac pump causes blood and fluid to back up, leading to oedema and pulmonary congestion
- **Stroke:** ischaemia or haemorrhage reduces oxygen delivery to brain tissue and causes neurological impairment
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis:** insulin deficiency causes ketone production, metabolic acidosis, hyperglycaemia and dehydration
- **Type 2 diabetes:** insulin resistance causes persistent hyperglycaemia and vascular damage
- **Obesity:** adipose tissue contributes to systemic inflammation and insulin resistance
- **Sepsis:** systemic infection causes inflammation, poor perfusion and possible organ dysfunction

## General Intervention Principles

### Independent nursing interventions

- Perform and repeat ABCDE assessments
- Monitor vital signs, oxygen saturation, neurological status and blood glucose as indicated
- Position the patient to support breathing and circulation
- Assess pain, hydration, fluid balance, oedema and respiratory effort
- Encourage coughing, deep breathing and effective breathing techniques when appropriate
- Maintain safety and protect the airway in patients with reduced consciousness
- Recognise deterioration and escalate promptly
- Educate patients about diet, exercise, glucose management and complication prevention

### Collaborative interventions

- Administer oxygen therapy, antibiotics, bronchodilators, insulin, IV fluids, diuretics, antiplatelet medication, vasodilators, analgesia or thrombolytics as prescribed and clinically indicated
- Obtain and monitor ECGs, arterial blood gases, blood glucose levels and electrolyte results
- Work with the medical team to manage infection, shock, respiratory failure, cardiac complications and neurological emergencies
- Escalate care for worsening oxygenation, hypotension, altered consciousness, seizures, arrhythmias or signs of organ dysfunction
