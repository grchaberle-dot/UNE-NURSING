# Haematological Disorders

**Source:** James Cook University NS2882 Nursing Practice 4 (Studocu). Australian.

---

## Blood Composition

**Plasma:** water, proteins (fibrinogen, globulins, albumin), nutrients, hormones, electrolytes.

**Formed elements:** red blood cells, white blood cells (monocytes, basophils, eosinophils, neutrophils, lymphocytes), platelets. WBCs plus platelets form the **buffy coat**.

---

## Erythrocyte Physiology

- **No nucleus**, which creates more space for haemoglobin
- Thin flexible membranes allow passage through narrow capillaries
- **Haemoglobin:** red pigment protein with four polypeptide chains (two alpha, two beta). **Iron** within haemoglobin binds oxygen
- Transports oxygen from lungs to tissues and assists CO2 transport (mainly after conversion to bicarbonate)
- Also helps **buffer blood pH**
- **Erythropoiesis:** RBC production occurs in **red bone marrow** and requires protein, minerals and vitamins
- **Lifespan:** approximately **120 days** before being broken down

---

## Anaemia

A deficiency in the number of erythrocytes, or in the quantity or quality of haemoglobin. The resulting reduction in oxygen-carrying capacity causes **tissue hypoxia** and may indicate an underlying disorder.

### General clinical manifestations

- Fatigue, weakness, tiredness, reduced activity tolerance
- Pallor
- Dizziness
- Shortness of breath, particularly on exertion
- Tachycardia, palpitations, tachypnoea or dyspnoea as compensatory responses
- Depending on cause: jaundice, pain, hypotension or signs of shock

### Diagnosis

- Full blood count
- Reticulocyte count
- Blood smear
- Cause-specific tests (serum vitamin or iron levels)

### Three main causes

1. Reduced red blood cell production
2. Blood loss
3. Increased destruction (haemolysis)

---

## Anaemia Types

| Type | Cause and pathophysiology | Clinical and diagnostic findings | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iron-deficiency** | Inadequate iron reduces haemoglobin synthesis, producing smaller erythrocytes | Fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea, dizziness. Low Hb, MCV, serum iron and ferritin. Pale small RBCs on film | Oral iron such as ferrous sulfate. IM or IV iron when oral not tolerated. Adverse effects: constipation, nausea, dark stools |
| **Thalassaemia** | Inherited mutations cause absent or reduced globin-chain production. Minor (one gene, mild), major (two genes, severe) | Major presents from early childhood with anaemia, impaired growth, jaundice, chronic bone-marrow hyperplasia. Low MCV and MCH, abnormal Hb patterns | Minor generally needs no treatment. Major may require regular transfusions and iron chelation (subcutaneous deferoxamine, oral deferasirox or deferiprone). Complications: cardiomyopathy, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Megaloblastic** | Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency impairs DNA synthesis, causing large immature fragile erythrocytes | Fatigue, weakness, pallor, dyspnoea. B12 deficiency also causes numbness, balance problems and neurological symptoms. Increased MCV | B12 replacement orally or intramuscularly. Folate replacement oral or parenteral (parenteral when malabsorption present) |
| **Aplastic** | Failure or destruction of pluripotential bone-marrow stem cells reduces production of erythrocytes, white blood cells **and** platelets | Fatigue, recurrent infections, bruising, bleeding. Causes include chemotherapy, toxins, infections | Stem-cell replacement through bone-marrow or peripheral-stem-cell transplantation, or immunosuppressive therapy |
| **Chronic disease** | Associated with chronic inflammation, CKD, heart failure, autoimmune disorders, cancer. Inflammatory cytokines increase **hepcidin**, reducing iron release and the erythropoietin response | Fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea, dizziness | Treat the underlying disease. Erythropoietin, iron supplementation, transfusion |

> Iron deficiency gives **small** cells (microcytic). B12 and folate deficiency gives **large** cells (macrocytic).

## Anaemia Caused by Blood Loss

### Acute blood loss

Sudden haemorrhage from trauma, surgery or internal bleeding. Large loss reduces circulating volume and may cause hypovolaemic shock.

**Important:** haemoglobin and haematocrit can initially appear **normal** because plasma and erythrocytes are lost together. Haemodilution becomes apparent after fluid replacement.

**Clinical findings:** tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, dizziness, fainting, weakness, cold clammy skin. Also vasovagal syncope, postural hypotension, hunger or feeling cold, shock, lactic acidosis.

**Management:** control the bleeding with compression or surgery, appropriate fluid management, blood transfusion if severe, oxygen if hypoxic.

### Chronic blood loss

Causes include excessive menstruation, bleeding ulcers, haemorrhoids. Gradual iron depletion causes iron-deficiency anaemia.

**Clinical findings:** fatigue, pallor, exertional dyspnoea, brittle nails, angular cheilitis, pica.

**Investigations and management:** FBC, iron studies, endoscopy, colonoscopy or urinalysis to identify the source. Treat the cause, replace iron, dietary advice.

## Haemolytic Anaemia

Haemolysis occurs when erythrocytes are destroyed faster than the bone marrow can replace them. The normal 120-day lifespan is shortened.

### Features of haemolysis

- Splenomegaly
- Anaemia
- Increased bone marrow activity
- **Haemoglobinaemia** (increased haemoglobin in blood)
- **Haemoglobinuria** (haemoglobin in urine)
- Jaundice
- Possible renal obstruction and tubular necrosis

### Types

- **Intrinsic (hereditary):** enzyme deficiencies, sickle cell disease, erythrocyte-membrane abnormalities
- **Extrinsic (acquired):** physical trauma during haemodialysis, cardiopulmonary bypass, passage through prosthetic heart valves. Also abnormal vessels, antibodies, infections, toxins

## Sickle Cell Disease

An inherited haemoglobinopathy (autosomal recessive) caused by a gene mutation producing abnormal haemoglobin.

**Triggers:** with **low oxygen levels, dehydration or acidosis**, abnormal haemoglobin polymerises and erythrocytes become rigid, sickle-shaped, fragile and sticky.

### Two problems

1. **Haemolysis:** abnormal erythrocytes destroyed particularly in the **spleen**, causing anaemia, jaundice, gallstones and possible splenomegaly
2. **Vaso-occlusion:** sickled cells obstruct small vessels, causing ischaemia, severe pain and organ damage

**Clinical manifestations:** fatigue, pallor, jaundice, gallstones, painful crises, swelling, chest pain, fever, priapism.

**Chronic complications:** delayed growth and puberty, leg ulcers, recurrent infections, pulmonary hypertension.

**Investigations:** FBC showing normocytic anaemia, blood film showing sickled cells, sickle-cell solubility screening.

**Management:** avoid triggers associated with low oxygen, prompt pain management, recognise and treat infections, prevent and manage hypoxia, maintain adequate fluid intake, blood transfusions when required, advise avoiding high altitudes, maintain immunisations, assess for and manage DVT. Consider stem-cell transplantation.

## Polycythaemia

An abnormal increase in erythrocytes. Increased blood viscosity raises the risk of thrombosis and stroke. May also increase circulating blood volume, producing hyperviscosity and hypervolaemia.

- **Primary:** a neoplastic disorder of pluripotent bone-marrow cells. Erythropoietin normal or reduced. White blood cells and platelets may also increase
- **Secondary:** increased erythropoietin stimulates erythrocyte production in response to hypoxia or ectopic erythropoietin production. Causes include chronic hypoxia, kidney disease, erythropoietin doping

**Clinical features:** headache, dizziness, tinnitus, blurred vision, pruritus, flushing or red complexion, fatigue, weakness.

**Complications:** thrombosis and gout.

**Management:** phlebotomy is the main treatment for primary disease. May also include agents to reduce blood viscosity and myelosuppressive treatment.

## Haemostasis: Four Stages

1. **Vascular response:** constriction of the injured vessel
2. **Primary haemostasis:** platelet adhesion and aggregation form an initial platelet plug. Assessed by **platelet count** and **bleeding time**
3. **Secondary haemostasis:** coagulation factors generate a fibrin clot. Assessed by **prothrombin time (PT)** and **activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)**
4. **Clot retraction and lysis:** the clot is stabilised and later broken down as the vessel is repaired

> Primary is platelets. Secondary is clotting factors. That split tells you which tests to order.

## Platelet Disorders

### Thrombocytopenia (low platelets)

Caused by reduced production (aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, chemotherapy) or increased immune destruction. Also viral infection, drug reactions, autoimmune disease, nutritional deficiencies, cancer, heparin-associated platelet reduction.

**Clinical findings:** mucosal bleeding, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, petechiae, purpura, superficial ecchymoses, prolonged bleeding. **Severe haemorrhage is the major complication.**

**Management:** treat the cause, platelet transfusion when required, bleeding precautions, monitor for bleeding.

### Thrombocytosis (high platelets)

Often reactive to infection, inflammation, trauma or iron deficiency. Usually asymptomatic but can cause thrombosis or haemorrhage.

### Qualitative platelet dysfunction

- **Inherited:** defective platelet-surface proteins or granule-storage defects
- **Acquired:** aspirin, NSAIDs, uraemia associated with renal failure

**Manifestations:** easy bruising, excessive bleeding, nosebleeds, gingival bleeding, petechiae.

**Management:** avoid medicines that worsen platelet function, consider platelet transfusion.

## Haemophilia

An inherited bleeding disorder caused by deficiency or dysfunction of coagulation factors. Impairs **secondary haemostasis** and produces an inadequate, unstable fibrin clot.

| Type | Deficiency | Inheritance |
|---|---|---|
| Haemophilia A | **Factor VIII**. Most common type | X-linked recessive. Affects males, females usually carriers |
| Haemophilia B | **Factor IX** | X-linked recessive |
| Haemophilia C | Factor XI (rare) | Autosomal recessive, affects both sexes |

**Clinical manifestations:** slow persistent bleeding after minor trauma, bleeding **into joints** causing pain swelling and restricted movement, easy bruising and muscle haematomas, epistaxis, haematuria, GI bleeding.

**Management:** coagulation-factor replacement therapy, desmopressin (increases clotting-factor release, useful in mild haemophilia A), **avoid aspirin and NSAIDs**, prevent injury, monitor carefully after procedures or trauma.

---

## Von Willebrand Disease

An inherited bleeding disorder caused by deficiency or dysfunction of **von Willebrand factor**, which is required for platelet adhesion and stabilisation of a coagulation factor. Described as autosomal dominant. Commonly affects mucous membranes.

**Clinical features:** easy bruising, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding, excessive bleeding after trauma.

**Management:** desmopressin to increase von Willebrand factor release, avoidance of aspirin and NSAIDs, hormonal therapy when appropriate, monitor for ongoing mucosal bleeding.

> Platelet problems bleed from mucous membranes and skin. Factor problems bleed deep into joints and muscles.

## Thromboembolic Disorders

Abnormal clots forming within blood vessels. A clot may obstruct local blood flow or detach and travel to another site.

### Virchow's triad

1. **Endothelial injury:** trauma, surgery, atherosclerosis, smoking
2. **Abnormal blood flow:** immobility, bed rest, long flights, varicose veins, atrial fibrillation. Includes stasis and turbulence
3. **Hypercoagulability:** inherited or acquired conditions, pregnancy, cancer, obesity, oral contraceptives, hormone replacement therapy, dehydration

### Venous thromboembolism

- **Deep-vein thrombosis:** a clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg. Calf pain, swelling, redness, warmth
- **Pulmonary embolism:** a clot, often from a deep vein, travels to the pulmonary arteries. Sudden dyspnoea, chest pain, haemoptysis, tachycardia. **Can be fatal**

### Arterial thrombosis

Arterial thrombi may form over ruptured atherosclerotic plaques or damaged endothelium. Can cause myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke or peripheral arterial disease.

### Management and prevention

- Anticoagulation
- Surgical clot removal if severe
- Thrombolysis for selected pulmonary emboli or ischaemic strokes
- Prevention through anticoagulants such as warfarin when indicated, compression socks, regular mobility, hydration, weight management

## Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

An acquired disorder involving widespread uncontrolled activation of coagulation. Microthrombi form throughout the circulation, consuming platelets and clotting factors. Fibrinolysis is also activated, so the patient may experience **thrombosis and bleeding simultaneously**.

### Causes and triggers

Sepsis and severe infection, widespread tissue damage, trauma, burns, tissue necrosis, neoplasia, haemolysis, severe transfusion reactions, drugs and toxins, uraemic syndrome, hypotension, hypovolaemic shock, hypoxia, sickle cell anaemia, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, metabolic acidosis, liver disease.

### Pathophysiology

1. A trigger releases **tissue factor** and activates the systemic coagulation cascade
2. Widespread fibrin deposition produces microthrombi and organ dysfunction
3. Platelets and clotting factors are **consumed** by the extensive clotting process
4. Increased fibrin breakdown produces bleeding because haemostatic components are depleted

### Clinical manifestations

- **Thrombotic effects:** respiratory difficulty or ARDS, ECG changes, venous distension, abdominal pain, kidney damage, cyanosis
- **Bleeding effects:** petechiae, purpura, haematomas, haemoptysis, hypotension, bloody stools, haematuria, dizziness, headache, bone or joint pain

### Diagnostic findings

Prolonged PT and aPTT, thrombocytopenia or rapidly falling platelet count, fibrin-degradation products, reduced coagulation inhibitors or clotting factors. Assessment of antithrombin and protein C, measurement of coagulation factors including V and X.

### Treatment

Identify and treat the underlying trigger, plasma and platelet replacement when indicated, consider anticoagulation in appropriate circumstances, monitor for **both** thrombosis and bleeding.

## Neutropenia

A reduction in neutrophils. Considered within **leucopenia** (reduction in white blood cells) or **granulocytopenia** (reduction in granulocytes).

### Nursing and infection-control considerations

- Use protective isolation when indicated
- Monitor closely for signs of infection
- Screen visitors according to local infection-control requirements
- Restrict or manage visitors who may transmit infection
- Follow strict infection-control procedures
- Administer prescribed medicines and monitor response

---

## Blood Transfusion Safety

### Blood products

Red blood cells, plasma, platelets, albumin, prothrombin products, cryoprecipitate.

### Before starting

- Ensure a suitable intravenous cannula or other approved access is available
- **Two nurses** should check the blood product according to local policy
- Check the expiry date
- Inspect colour and appearance
- Check for clumping, bubbles or other abnormalities
- Record required documentation: time commenced, access used, transfusion location
- **Record baseline observations before commencing**

### During and after

- Monitor observations before, during and after the infusion
- Observe for changes in the patient's condition
- Document the transfusion and the patient's response
- **Stop and report** suspected reactions according to local policy

### Possible transfusion reactions

- **Febrile reaction:** associated with fever or an increase in temperature
- **Hypersensitivity reaction:** an allergic response to the transfused product
- **Haemolytic reaction:** destruction of transfused or recipient red blood cells

## Key Distinctions for Revision

- **Decreased production anaemia:** insufficient or ineffective erythrocyte production (iron deficiency, thalassaemia, megaloblastic, aplastic, chronic disease)
- **Blood-loss anaemia:** erythrocytes lost through acute or chronic haemorrhage
- **Haemolytic anaemia:** erythrocytes destroyed prematurely
- **Thrombocytopenia:** too few platelets, primarily causing mucocutaneous bleeding
- **Thrombocytosis:** too many platelets, increasing thrombosis risk and sometimes haemorrhage
- **Platelet dysfunction:** platelet numbers may be normal but adhesion or aggregation is impaired
- **Haemophilia:** coagulation-factor deficiency causing impaired secondary haemostasis and deep tissue or joint bleeding
- **Von Willebrand disease:** impaired platelet adhesion and reduced stabilisation of a coagulation factor, commonly causing mucosal bleeding
- **Thromboembolism:** abnormal clot formation that may obstruct a vessel or travel to another site
- **DIC:** simultaneous widespread clotting, consumption of haemostatic factors, and bleeding

### Key clinical priorities

- Assess fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea, palpitations and other indicators of reduced oxygen-carrying capacity
- Identify whether anaemia is caused by reduced production, blood loss or destruction
- Monitor carefully for bleeding in platelet and coagulation disorders
- In neutropenia, prioritise infection prevention and early recognition
- In DIC, assess simultaneously for thrombosis and bleeding
- When administering blood products, complete all safety checks and monitor before, during and after
