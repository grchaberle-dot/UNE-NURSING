\uD83C\uDFE0 Environmental Safety Assessment

  • Adequate lighting in bedroom, bathroom, hallways and frequently used areas
  • Night-lights to improve visibility during night-time toileting
  • Remove clutter, loose rugs and unsecured electrical wires
  • Arrange IV tubing and equipment so pathways remain clear
  • Keep the bed low and lock the wheels
  • Use bed rails appropriately, avoiding entrapment or climbing hazards
  • Keep the call bell and personal items within reach
  • Install grab bars near the toilet and shower
  • Non-slip mats on wet surfaces, stable furniture
  • Store frequently used items where the patient can reach them without climbing or excessive bending

\uD83E\uDDCE Fall Prevention Plan

  • Scheduled rounding, assist with transfers and toileting
  • Ensure the walker is correctly fitted and consistently used
  • Teach the patient to request assistance before standing
  • Consider a bed alarm when clinically appropriate
  • Assess orthostatic vital signs, monitor for dizziness or weakness
  • Clear fall-risk signage, communicate precautions to all staff
  • Encourage strength, balance and mobility exercises through rehabilitation services
  • Develop a home-safety plan before discharge, especially if the patient lives alone

\uD83E\uDE79 Pressure Injury Prevention

  • Inspect the skin regularly, especially over bony prominences
  • Reposition the patient and use pressure-relieving devices
  • Keep skin clean and dry, particularly when incontinence is present
  • Provide adequate nutrition and hydration
  • Identify and treat early redness, warmth, tenderness or breakdown
Limited mobility plus incontinence is the classic high-risk combination. Both appear on the Braden Scale for a reason.

\uD83D\uDCAA Safe Manual Handling

Common causes of staff injury

  1. Incorrect lifting technique
  2. Poor posture when handling residents
  3. Incorrect adjustment or movement of equipment
  4. Attempting to lift objects or people that are too heavy
  5. Managing a stressed, violent or confused person alone

Key principles

  • Lifting: keep the spine straight, engage the abdominal muscles, lift from a stable position. Force comes from the legs, not the back.
  • Pushing: generally safer than lifting or pulling. Use body weight, a wide base of support and the lower limbs. Keep elbows close to the waist.
  • Pulling: distribute body weight evenly through the feet, face the object directly, use both arms, initiate movement with the lower limbs.
  • Assistance: never attempt to manage a heavy, distressed or unpredictable person alone. Get help and use suitable equipment.
If you injure your back you are out of the workforce. Manual handling injuries are among the most common reasons nurses leave clinical practice early.

\uD83D\uDC69 Case Study: Complex Safety Risk

Patient profile

A woman with atrial fibrillation, diabetes, congestive heart failure, COPD, hypertension, a history of falls and a hip fracture caused by a fall. She lives alone, uses a walker inconsistently, has mild cognitive impairment, and takes multiple medications.

Primary safety risks

  • Falls: previous falls, hip fracture, impaired mobility, inconsistent walker use, possible dizziness, hypotension, weakness and cognitive impairment
  • Pressure injury: limited mobility and incontinence
  • Medication harm: polypharmacy, anticoagulation, diuresis, hypotension, hypoglycaemia and drug interactions
  • Cognitive and ADL limitations: memory and judgement difficulty increases dependence and injury risk
  • Living alone: delayed assistance during a fall, medication problem or medical emergency

Medication safety considerations

WarfarinBleeding risk. Monitor bruising, bleeding, falls, and medication or dietary interactions.

Anticoagulation plus a high falls risk is a genuine clinical tension. Warfarin reduces stroke risk in atrial fibrillation, but a fall on warfarin carries a serious bleeding risk. This is exactly the kind of trade-off that needs documenting and escalating, not resolving alone.

\uD83C\uDFE1 Discharge & Support Planning

  • Arrange home-health services or a caregiver for ADLs, medication administration and safety monitoring
  • Consider remote monitoring for weight, symptoms, blood pressure or glucose where clinically indicated
  • Provide an emergency plan with contact information and instructions for an emergency-alert system
  • Involve family, friends, neighbours and community resources where available
  • Provide meal planning, hydration support, medication education, cognitive stimulation and psychosocial support
  • Use simple language, written instructions, visual aids and teach-back to confirm understanding
For a patient with a high falls score, hip fracture, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy and limited ADLs, prioritise immediate safety, assisted mobility, medication review, skin protection and a reliable discharge-support network.
