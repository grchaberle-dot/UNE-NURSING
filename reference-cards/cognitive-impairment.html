\uD83E\uDDE0 Cognitive Impairment & Legal Orders

Dementia, Delirium, Capacity, Restraint & Legal Frameworks

\u2190 Back to Hub
Educational resource only. Legal processes vary between jurisdictions. NSW uses NCAT (not SACAT), Guardianship Act 1987 (NSW), and Mental Health Act 2007 (NSW). Always follow your facility's policies.

\uD83E\uDDE0 Three Main Causes of Cognitive Impairment

\uD83E\uDDE0 Dementia

  • Progressive and usually irreversible
  • Affects memory, language, behaviour, judgment
  • Types: Alzheimer's (most common), Vascular, Lewy Body, Frontotemporal
  • Gradual onset over months to years
  • Capacity may fluctuate early; typically declines
  • Communication adaptations: short sentences, visual cues, familiar faces

\u26A1 Delirium

  • Acute onset: hours to days
  • Fluctuating consciousness, disorientation
  • Usually reversible with treatment of cause
  • Common causes: infection, meds, pain, urinary retention, constipation, dehydration, post-op
  • Screen with 4AT or CAM tools
  • Non-pharmacological management first: reorient, familiar environment, mobility

\uD83E\uDDF8 Acquired Brain Injury

  • Occurs after birth: stroke, TBI, hypoxia, infection
  • Variable deficits depending on injury location
  • May affect memory, attention, impulse control, language
  • Can be stable or improving with rehab
  • Deficits often domain-specific
  • Collaborate with OT, speech path, neuro rehab
Delirium is REVERSIBLE. Dementia is not. Always rule out delirium first in an acutely confused patient. The 4AT takes under 2 minutes.

\u2696\uFE0F Assessing Decision-Making Capacity

Key principle: Capacity is DECISION-SPECIFIC and TIME-SPECIFIC. A person may have capacity for some decisions but not others. Always presume capacity unless there is evidence otherwise.

Four Functional Abilities (all must be present)

  1. Understand: Can they explain the information in their own words?
  2. Retain: Can they hold it long enough to decide? (Not indefinitely)
  3. Weigh up: Can they consider risks, benefits, and consequences?
  4. Communicate: Can they express their choice in any form (verbal, written, gesture, AAC)?
Impaired capacity \u2260 refusing treatment. A person without capacity cannot legally consent, but treatment doesn't proceed automatically. A substitute decision-maker (SDM) must be identified.

\uD83D\uDCCB Legal Documents

Advance Care Directive (ACD)

  • Records wishes, values, and goals for future healthcare
  • Can appoint a substitute decision-maker
  • Can include binding refusals of treatment
  • Must be made while person has capacity
  • Supersedes family preferences when in conflict
  • NSW: similar under common law and specific health legislation

Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA)

  • Appoints someone for financial/property decisions
  • Does NOT automatically cover healthcare decisions
  • Check what the document actually authorises
  • Ceases on death

Enduring Power of Guardianship (EPG)

  • Appoints a guardian for personal/lifestyle decisions
  • Where the person lives, healthcare, who they associate with
  • Activated only when person lacks capacity
  • Guardian must act in person's best interests
  • NSW: Enduring Guardian under Guardianship Act 1987

\uD83D\uDEAB Restraint: Types & Reduction

"Restraint is the restriction of an individual's freedom of movement." (ACSQHC)

Includes: mechanical (devices), physical (hands-on force), and chemical/pharmacological (medication to sedate or control behaviour).

Things that count as restraint: PRN psychotropics, bed rails, ITOs, Code Black/Security Assist, physical restraint devices.

Improper restraint can cause falls, skin tears, pressure injuries, aspiration, increased agitation, and psychological harm. Restraint is always a LAST RESORT.

\uD83D\uDC8A Medication & Pain

  • Understand benefits/risks of common psychotropic meds
  • Identify and manage withdrawals (nicotine, alcohol, opioids, benzos)
  • Use accurate pain tools: PAIN-AD for non-verbal patients
  • Use analgesia first if known painful condition
  • Unmanaged pain is a common driver of agitation

\uD83D\uDCAC De-escalation

  • Attend Safety Intervention training at your facility
  • Behaviour is often communication
  • Identify early signs of agitation: intervene before escalation
  • Calm voice, non-threatening posture, give space
  • Offer choices and validate feelings
  • Involve family or familiar people

\uD83D\uDEA8 Wandering Patient Safety (11-Step Checklist)

  1. Confirm patient is at risk (history, diagnosis, current behaviour)
  2. Speak to family/carer, obtain consent, document conversation
  3. Move patient to room away from exit doors
  4. Obtain wandering alarm & wristband from Clinical Manager
  5. Explain alarm to patient (as able), document attempt
  6. Ensure all staff know what patient looks like (verbal handover)
  7. Document patient's clothing in nursing notes (update each shift)
  8. Add 'Absconder' alert to patient management system
  9. 'Keep Door Closed' signs on fire doors and main ward doors
  10. Sign on patient's room door (with name, per facility policy)
  11. Sign on patient's toilet door
Document EVERY step with date, time, and your name. If patient is actively missing, follow your facility's Missing Patient Protocol immediately. This checklist is for PREVENTION.

\uD83D\uDCCA Australia Dementia Stats (AIHW)

  • 425,000 Australians living with dementia
  • 65% are women
  • 1.1 million estimated by 2065
  • 68% live in the community (not in care)
  • 25% born in a non-English speaking country
  • 2023: Leading cause of death in Australia (1 in 10 deaths)
  • Second leading cause of disease burden
  • 6 modifiable risk factors contribute to 43% of cases
  • People with dementia receive less pain relief than others with the same conditions
