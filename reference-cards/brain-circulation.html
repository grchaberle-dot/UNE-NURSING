\uD83D\uDEE1\uFE0F Structures That Protect the Brain

Cranium

The strong bony skull encloses and protects the brain from mechanical forces.

Meninges

Three connective-tissue membranes covering the brain and spinal cord.

Cerebrospinal Fluid

A fluid cushion that supports and protects neural tissue.

Blood-Brain Barrier

A selective barrier limiting passage of harmful substances and pathogens from blood into brain tissue.

Protective sequence from outside in: cranium \u2192 dura mater \u2192 arachnoid mater \u2192 subarachnoid space with CSF \u2192 pia mater \u2192 blood-brain barrier.

\uD83E\uDDEC The Three Meningeal Layers

LayerCharacteristics and Function
Dura materOutermost, tough, leathery. Cranial dura has periosteal and meningeal layers. The layers separate in places to form dural sinuses.
Arachnoid materMiddle membrane. The subarachnoid space beneath it contains major cerebral blood vessels and CSF.
Pia materThin, delicate inner membrane that closely follows the brain surface. Contains capillaries that nourish neural tissue.
Outside to inside: Dura, Arachnoid, Pia. Think DAP. Dura is tough, Arachnoid is web-like with the CSF space, Pia hugs the brain.

\u26A0\uFE0F Meningeal Spaces & Clinical Significance

  • Subdural space: Narrow space between dura and arachnoid, containing a thin film of fluid and draining veins.
  • Subarachnoid space: Larger space containing CSF and major cerebral blood vessels.
  • Subdural haemorrhage: Blood accumulates in the subdural space after vessel rupture.
  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage: Blood accumulates in the subarachnoid space after vessel rupture.
  • Arachnoid villi: Projections that absorb CSF into venous blood within the dural sinuses.
  • Meningitis: Inflammation of the meninges, commonly bacterial, viral or fungal. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency.
Meningitis triad: headache, fever, stiff neck. Suspected bacterial meningitis needs urgent medical attention.

\uD83D\uDCA7 Cerebrospinal Fluid

CSF is derived from blood plasma and produced in the four interconnected ventricles.

Circulation Pathway

  1. Produced in the four ventricles
  2. Flows through the ventricles
  3. Circulates through the subarachnoid space around brain and spinal cord
  4. Passes through the central canal of the spinal cord
  5. Returns to venous blood through the arachnoid villi

Functions

  • Acts as a liquid cushion against mechanical injury
  • Helps suspend the brain, reducing the effective downward force of its weight
  • Provides a diffusion medium for nutrients and waste removal

\uD83D\uDEAA Blood-Brain Barrier

Formed mainly by tight junctions between endothelial cells of brain capillaries. These capillaries are among the least permeable in the body.

More readily permeable to

  • Oxygen and carbon dioxide
  • Lipids
  • Alcohol and nicotine
  • Anaesthetics
  • Glucose

Relatively impermeable to

  • Many metabolic wastes
  • Proteins and toxins
  • Potassium ions
  • Many drugs and antibiotics
Medication delivery to the brain depends on whether the substance can cross the blood-brain barrier. This is why some antibiotics are useless for CNS infections.

\u2764\uFE0F Arterial Blood Supply

  1. The right and left internal carotid arteries ascend through the neck
  2. The right and left vertebral arteries ascend at the back of the neck
  3. Each internal carotid gives rise to an anterior cerebral artery and a middle cerebral artery
  4. The vertebral arteries unite to form the basilar artery
  5. The basilar artery divides into the right and left posterior cerebral arteries

\uD83D\uDD04 The Circle of Willis

The cerebral arterial circle is an arterial anastomosis at the base of the cerebrum. It provides alternate routes for blood flow if one major artery becomes narrowed or occluded.

Components

  • Right and left anterior cerebral arteries
  • Anterior communicating artery
  • Right and left internal carotid arteries
  • Right and left posterior cerebral arteries
  • Right and left posterior communicating arteries
The Circle of Willis is the brain's built-in redundancy. It's why some people survive an occluded artery with minimal deficit: collateral circulation keeps the tissue perfused.

\uD83D\uDD35 Venous Drainage

Cerebral veins drain deoxygenated blood into dural sinuses. Blood then passes toward the heart through the internal jugular veins. The sigmoid sinuses are part of this venous drainage pathway.
