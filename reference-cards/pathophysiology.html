\uD83D\uDCA7 Heart Failure

The heart cannot pump effectively, so blood backs up behind the failing side.

Left-sided failure

Blood backs up into the lungs.

  • Shortness of breath at rest and on exertion
  • Orthopnoea: needs to sit up to breathe
  • Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea: waking feeling like drowning
  • Crackles on auscultation

Right-sided failure

Blood backs up into the body.

  • Peripheral oedema
  • Weight gain from fluid retention
  • Jugular vein distension
Left equals Lungs. Right equals the Rest of the body. A rapid weight gain (like 5kg in a month) is fluid, not fat.

Assessing respiratory distress

  • Respiratory rate, depth and effort
  • Accessory muscle use
  • Ability to speak in full sentences
  • SpO2 and oxygen requirement
  • Auscultation: wheeze, crackles, diminished or absent air entry
A silent chest in severe asthma is ominous, not reassuring. No air movement means no wheeze.

\uD83E\uDDD2 Paediatric Respiratory Differences

Children decompensate faster because:

  • Smaller airways: a small amount of swelling causes proportionally much greater obstruction
  • Relatively larger tongue
  • Higher metabolic rate, so higher oxygen demand
  • Faster baseline respiratory and heart rates
A child's normal observations are not an adult's. Faster rates are expected, so what looks like tachycardia may be normal for their age.

\u26A1 Shock

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion. Four types:

TypeMechanismExamples
HypovolaemicLoss of circulating volumeHaemorrhage, dehydration, burns
CardiogenicThe heart fails to pump effectivelyLarge MI
DistributiveWidespread vasodilationSeptic, anaphylactic, neurogenic
ObstructivePhysical obstruction to flowTamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE
Compensated shock looks deceptively stable. A rising heart rate with a still-normal blood pressure means the body is working hard to compensate. It is a warning, not reassurance.

\uD83E\uDDA0 Sepsis

A dysregulated host response to infection causing organ dysfunction. Untreated it progresses to septic shock and multi-organ dysfunction.

  • Fever or hypothermia
  • Tachycardia and tachypnoea
  • Hypotension
  • Altered conscious state
  • Reduced urine output
In older adults a modest temperature rise may signal serious infection, because their baseline temperature response is blunted. Do not wait for a dramatic fever.

\uD83C\uDF6C Diabetic Ketoacidosis

Insufficient insulin means glucose cannot enter cells, so the body breaks down fat, producing ketones and acidosis.

  • Hyperglycaemia
  • Ketosis and metabolic acidosis
  • Polyuria and polydipsia leading to dehydration
  • Electrolyte disturbance

Management centres on fluid replacement, insulin and careful electrolyte correction. Both 0.9% sodium chloride and Lactated Ringer's appear as fluid options in DKA.

Always check a blood glucose in any patient with altered conscious state. Hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia can both present as confusion.

\uD83E\uDDE0 Stroke

Ischaemic

A clot blocks a cerebral vessel, starving tissue of oxygen.

Haemorrhagic

A vessel ruptures and bleeds into or around the brain.

FAST: Face drooped, Arms cannot lift, Speech slurred, Time critical.

Management differs completely between the two types, so imaging must come before treatment. Never assume which one it is.
The single most valuable thing you can hand over is the TIME of symptom onset. Treatment windows depend on it.

\uD83E\uDEB8 Chronic Kidney Disease

Progressive, irreversible loss of kidney function, staged by eGFR. As function declines:

  • Waste products accumulate (rising urea and creatinine)
  • Fluid balance is impaired, causing overload
  • Electrolytes become deranged, particularly potassium
  • Acid-base balance is disturbed
Nephrotoxic medications, including NSAIDs and some antibiotics, can worsen renal function. A rising creatinine with a falling eGFR should prompt a medication review.
Hyperkalaemia is the emergency to watch in renal failure, because the kidneys are the main route for potassium excretion.

\uD83D\uDD25 Burns

Burns cause fluid shift out of the vessels, which is why burn patients need aggressive fluid resuscitation and are at risk of hypovolaemic shock.

  • Assess extent using a body surface area method such as the Rule of Nines
  • Assess depth
  • Fluid resuscitation is calculated from weight and burn percentage
  • Monitor for airway involvement in facial or inhalation burns
Airway burns can swell rapidly. Any facial burn, singed nasal hair or hoarse voice is an airway emergency.
