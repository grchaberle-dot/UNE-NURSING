\uD83D\uDD24 The ABCDE Primary Survey

A

Airway

Is the airway patent? Look for obstruction, stridor, gurgling or snoring. Can they speak in full sentences? A patient who cannot maintain their own airway is the highest priority in the room.

B

Breathing

Respiratory rate, depth and effort. Accessory muscle use. SpO2 and oxygen requirement. Auscultate for crackles, wheeze or absent air entry. Symmetry of chest movement.

C

Circulation

Heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure, capillary refill, peripheral pulses, skin colour and temperature. Urine output. Any bleeding.

D

Disability

Level of consciousness (GCS or AVPU), pupils, blood glucose, limb movement and power, pain score.

E

Exposure

Temperature, full skin inspection, wounds, drains, lines, rashes, oedema. Maintain dignity and warmth while you look.

Work ABCDE in order every single time, and fix what you find before moving on. A problem at A always outranks a problem at E.

\uD83E\uDE7A DRSABCD Basic Life Support

LetterAction
DDanger: check for hazards to yourself, others and the patient
RResponse: check for a response (talk and touch)
SSend for help: activate the emergency response
AAirway: open and clear the airway
BBreathing: look, listen and feel for normal breathing
CCPR: start compressions and rescue breaths
DDefibrillation: attach a defibrillator as soon as available
Danger and Response come before anything clinical. You are no use to the patient if you become the second casualty.

\uD83D\uDCC8 Recognising Clinical Deterioration

Observation charts are designed with coloured escalation zones. A single observation in the escalation zone, or a trend heading that way, requires action.

What to watch for

  • Trend, not just the number: three sets of obs each slightly worse is deterioration even if none has hit the red zone
  • Respiratory rate: often the earliest and most sensitive sign of deterioration, and the most commonly missed
  • Reduced conscious state: any new confusion, drowsiness or agitation
  • Falling urine output
  • Patient or family concern: "something is not right" is a legitimate escalation trigger
  • Your own worry: if you are worried, that alone justifies escalating
Respiratory rate is the vital sign most often skipped and the one that changes earliest. Count it properly for a full 60 seconds.

\uD83D\uDCDE Escalating Care

  1. Stay with the patient and get someone else to make the call if you can
  2. Reassess using ABCDE while help is coming
  3. Call according to your facility's protocol: nurse in charge, medical review, Rapid Response or MET call
  4. Hand over using ISBAR with a specific recommendation
  5. Document the time, who you called, what you reported and the agreed plan
  6. Escalate again if the response is inadequate or the patient keeps deteriorating
You never need permission to escalate. Escalating early and being told everything is fine is always better than escalating late.

\u26A1 Recognising Shock

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion. The four main types:

TypeMechanism
HypovolaemicLoss of circulating volume: haemorrhage, dehydration, burns
CardiogenicThe heart fails to pump effectively
DistributiveWidespread vasodilation: septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic
ObstructivePhysical obstruction to flow: tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE
Shock progresses through stages. Early compensated shock can look deceptively stable because the body is compensating hard. A rising heart rate with a still-normal blood pressure is a warning, not reassurance.

\uD83E\uDDE0 Stroke: FAST

LetterCheck
FFace: has their face drooped?
AArms: can they lift both arms?
SSpeech: is their speech slurred? Do they understand you?
TTime: time is critical. Note the time of onset and act immediately.

Stroke types: ischaemic (clot) and haemorrhagic (bleed). Management differs completely, so imaging comes before treatment.

Time of symptom ONSET is the single most important piece of information you can hand over in a suspected stroke.

\uD83D\uDCCB Neurological Assessment in Deterioration

  • GCS: eye opening, verbal response, motor response
  • Pupils: size, equality and reaction to light (PERRLA)
  • Limb power: compare both sides using the 0 to 5 muscle strength scale
  • Blood glucose: always check it in any altered conscious state
  • Blood pressure: monitor carefully post-stroke, following the specific parameters ordered
Hypoglycaemia can mimic stroke and many other neurological presentations. Check a blood glucose on every patient with altered consciousness.
