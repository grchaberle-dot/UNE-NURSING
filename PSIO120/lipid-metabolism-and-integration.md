## Lipid Functions and Types

Lipids are relatively insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents. They provide concentrated energy, store energy without associated water, form membranes, insulate and protect organs, carry fat-soluble vitamins, and act as signalling precursors.

### Fatty acids

- **Saturated:** no double bonds
- **Monounsaturated:** one double bond
- **Polyunsaturated:** several double bonds

Unsaturated **cis** double bonds introduce bends, lower melting point and increase membrane fluidity. **Trans fats** are produced during food processing and behave more like saturated fats.

### Other lipids

- **Triacylglycerols:** glycerol esterified to three fatty acids. Non-polar, hydrophobic. Major storage form in adipocytes
- **Phospholipids:** fatty acids, glycerol, phosphate and an alcohol group. Amphipathic structure makes them suitable for membranes
- **Cholesterol:** amphipathic sterol. Supports membrane integrity and fluidity. Precursor for sex hormones, cortisol, bile acids and vitamin D

**Fat-soluble vitamins:** A, D, E, K. Vitamin D is formed from cholesterol after UV exposure and supports calcium and phosphorus absorption.

## Dietary Lipid Digestion

1. In infants, lingual and gastric lipases act on medium-chain triglycerides in milk
2. In adults, mechanical mixing creates fat globules
3. **Bile acids** from liver and gallbladder emulsify fats in the small intestine
4. **Pancreatic lipase** hydrolyses triacylglycerols to free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols
5. These products form **micelles** with bile acids and enter intestinal epithelial cells
6. Inside enterocytes, triglycerides are resynthesised and packaged into **chylomicrons**
7. Chylomicrons enter the **lymphatic system** before reaching the bloodstream

> Fat enters the lymphatics first, not the blood. That is why lymph turns cloudy after a fatty meal and is called chyle.

---

## Lipoproteins

Make hydrophobic lipids transportable in blood. Core contains triacylglycerols and cholesteryl esters. Surface contains phospholipids, free cholesterol and apoproteins.

| Particle | Major role |
|---|---|
| Chylomicron | Transports dietary triglycerides from intestine to muscle and adipose tissue |
| Chylomicron remnant | Triglyceride-depleted particle taken up by liver |
| VLDL | Transports liver-derived triglycerides to peripheral tissues |
| IDL | Intermediate particle formed after triglyceride removal from VLDL |
| LDL | Delivers cholesterol to tissues. Cleared by the liver |
| HDL | Participates in return of excess cholesterol to the liver |

**Lipoprotein lipase** on capillary surfaces hydrolyses triglycerides in chylomicrons and VLDL, releasing fatty acids. **Hepatic lipase** processes remnant particles and IDL.

> LDL loses cholesterol into arteries. HDL hauls it back to the liver.

## Mobilisation During Fasting

Falling blood glucose increases **glucagon** and **adrenaline**. These activate **hormone-sensitive lipase** in adipose tissue, releasing free fatty acids and glycerol. Free fatty acids travel bound to **serum albumin**. Glycerol enters glycolysis or gluconeogenesis after conversion to an intermediate.

---

## Activation and the Carnitine Shuttle

Fatty acids are activated by **acyl-CoA synthetase** at the outer mitochondrial membrane.

**Fatty acid + ATP + CoA -> fatty acyl-CoA + AMP + PPi**

Because ATP converts to AMP, activation costs the equivalent of **two ATP**.

Long-chain fatty acyl-CoA cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane directly. The **carnitine shuttle** transfers the acyl group via carnitine acyltransferase I, the translocase, and carnitine acyltransferase II.

**Malonyl-CoA inhibits carnitine acyltransferase I**, preventing simultaneous fatty-acid synthesis and oxidation.

---

## Beta Oxidation

Occurs in the **mitochondrial matrix**. Removes two carbon atoms per cycle as acetyl-CoA.

1. **Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase** oxidises the chain, producing reduced flavin cofactor (FADH2)
2. **Enoyl-CoA hydratase** adds water across the double bond
3. **Hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase** oxidises the hydroxyl group, producing NADH
4. **Thiolase** cleaves the bond and releases acetyl-CoA

**Palmitate (16-carbon):** seven cycles produce 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 FADH2 and 7 NADH. Including TCA oxidation and subtracting activation cost gives a net yield of approximately **106 ATP**.

> Fatty acids yield more ATP per carbon and per gram than glucose because they are more reduced.

---

## Fatty-Acid Synthesis

Occurs mainly in the **cytosol** of liver, adipose tissue, lactating mammary tissue and the CNS. Promoted when carbohydrate and energy are abundant.

1. Acetyl-CoA transferred from mitochondria to cytosol as citrate
2. Citrate cleaved to acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate
3. Acetyl-CoA carboxylated to malonyl-CoA
4. **Fatty-acid synthase** repeatedly adds two-carbon units using NADPH

**Committed step:** Acetyl-CoA + HCO3- + ATP -> malonyl-CoA + ADP + Pi

**Acetyl-CoA carboxylase** uses biotin, is stimulated by citrate, and is inhibited by palmitoyl-CoA through feedback inhibition.

Mammals have limited desaturase capacity, which explains the dietary requirement for some omega fatty acids.

## Ketone Bodies

### Production

During prolonged fasting or uncontrolled diabetes, gluconeogenesis consumes **oxaloacetate**. As a result, acetyl-CoA from fatty-acid oxidation cannot enter the TCA cycle efficiently. In **liver mitochondria**, excess acetyl-CoA is converted into ketone bodies:

- **Acetoacetate**
- **Beta-hydroxybutyrate**
- **Acetone**

Ketone bodies are water-soluble and can be exported in the blood. Acetoacetate spontaneously decarboxylates to acetone, producing the characteristic **fruity breath odour**.

### Use

Heart muscle, renal cortex, skeletal muscle and, during prolonged starvation, the **brain** can use ketone bodies. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is oxidised to acetoacetate producing NADH. Acetoacetate receives CoA from succinyl-CoA and is cleaved to two acetyl-CoA.

**The liver produces ketone bodies but cannot use them**, because it lacks the relevant CoA-transferase.

Ketone bodies conserve glucose and reduce the need to degrade muscle protein during prolonged starvation. Excessive production causes **ketoacidosis**, particularly in uncontrolled type 1 diabetes.

## Gluconeogenesis

Synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. Occurs mainly in the **liver**, to a lesser extent the **kidneys**. Important when dietary glucose is unavailable and liver glycogen is depleted.

### Precursors

- Pyruvate
- Lactate
- Glycerol
- TCA-cycle intermediates
- Carbon skeletons of most amino acids

All precursors must ultimately form **oxaloacetate**. **Leucine and lysine are not glucogenic** because their degradation produces only acetyl units. **Most fatty acids cannot provide net glucose** because they degrade mainly to acetyl-CoA.

### Bypass reactions

Seven glycolytic reactions are reversible. Three irreversible reactions need alternative enzymes:

1. **Pyruvate to phosphoenolpyruvate:** pyruvate carboxylase converts pyruvate to oxaloacetate in the mitochondrion using biotin. Oxaloacetate transported as malate, then decarboxylated by PEP carboxykinase
2. **Fructose bisphosphate to fructose phosphate:** fructose bisphosphatase removes a phosphate by hydrolysis
3. **Glucose phosphate to glucose:** glucose-6-phosphatase removes the final phosphate in liver and kidney

Producing one glucose from two pyruvate consumes **six high-energy phosphate equivalents**. Energetically expensive.

---

## The Cori Cycle

1. During anaerobic muscle activity, glucose is converted to **lactate**
2. Lactate travels through the blood to the **liver**
3. Liver converts lactate to glucose by gluconeogenesis
4. Glucose returns to muscle

Supports rapid ATP production but consumes more energy in the liver than the muscle gains through glycolysis.

---

## Reciprocal Regulation

Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis must not proceed rapidly at the same time because this wastes ATP. **Fructose bisphosphate** strongly activates phosphofructokinase and inhibits fructose bisphosphatase, coordinating opposite pathway fluxes. ATP, AMP, ADP, citrate, insulin and glucagon also regulate the balance.

---

## Urea Cycle

Occurs partly in the mitochondrial matrix and partly in the cytosol of **liver cells**.

1. **Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase:** combines ammonium, bicarbonate and two ATP to form carbamoyl phosphate in the mitochondrion
2. **Ornithine transcarbamylase:** transfers the carbamoyl group to ornithine, forming citrulline
3. **Argininosuccinate synthetase:** combines citrulline with aspartate. Aspartate provides the second nitrogen. ATP cleaved to AMP and pyrophosphate
4. **Argininosuccinate lyase:** cleaves argininosuccinate into arginine and fumarate
5. **Arginase:** hydrolyses arginine to urea and ornithine. Ornithine returns to the mitochondrion

The two nitrogens in urea come from **ammonium** and **aspartate**. Four high-energy phosphate equivalents are consumed.

**Urea-cycle defects cause hyperammonaemia**, which can produce irreversible brain damage.

### Removing amino groups

- **Transamination:** aminotransferases transfer the alpha-amino group to alpha-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate. Require pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6 derivative)
- **Oxidative deamination:** glutamate dehydrogenase in the mitochondrial matrix releases ammonium from glutamate and regenerates alpha-ketoglutarate

### Muscle nitrogen transport

Muscle lacks the complete urea cycle, so nitrogen must be transported to the liver:

- **Alanine cycle:** amino groups transferred to pyruvate forming alanine. Alanine travels to the liver where its carbon skeleton supports gluconeogenesis
- **Glutamine transport:** glutamate combines with ammonium using glutamine synthetase. Glutamine travels to the liver where its nitrogen enters the urea cycle

### Glucogenic vs ketogenic amino acids

- **Glucogenic:** carbon skeletons form pyruvate or TCA intermediates, supporting gluconeogenesis
- **Ketogenic:** carbon skeletons form acetyl-CoA or related compounds, producing ketone bodies

## Fasting and Starvation: Six Stages

1. Blood glucose is used first
2. **Liver glycogen** supports glucose maintenance through the early fasting period
3. **Gluconeogenesis** begins as glycogen declines
4. **Adipose lipolysis** supplies fatty acids and glycerol
5. **Fatty-acid oxidation** supplies ATP for gluconeogenesis
6. **Ketone bodies** become increasingly important during prolonged starvation

The body conserves glucose for tissues that depend strongly on it, especially **red blood cells** and initially the **brain**. During prolonged starvation the brain adapts to ketone bodies, reducing the need to degrade muscle protein. **Fat stores are a major determinant of survival time.**

## Tissue Specialisation

| Tissue | Key features |
|---|---|
| Liver | GLUT2, glucokinase, glucose-6-phosphatase, gluconeogenic, lipogenic and ketogenic enzymes. Releases glucose. Cannot use ketone bodies |
| Skeletal muscle | GLUT4 and hexokinase. Stores glycogen for local use. Lacks glucose-6-phosphatase |
| Cardiac muscle | Heavily aerobic. Can use fatty acids, glucose, lactate and ketones |
| Adipose tissue | GLUT4. Stores fatty acids as triacylglycerols |
| Brain | Glucose via GLUT1 and GLUT3. Ketone bodies during prolonged starvation |
| Red blood cells | No mitochondria. Glycolysis only, using GLUT1 and hexokinase |

## Hormonal Control of Metabolism

### Signal transduction

1. **Reception:** a ligand binds a receptor
2. **Transduction:** the signal is converted into intracellular chemical events
3. **Response:** enzyme activity, transport, gene expression or cell behaviour changes

### Receptor types

- **Seven-transmembrane receptors** (glucagon, beta-adrenergic): ligand binding promotes GDP for GTP exchange on a heterotrimeric G protein. Activated G protein stimulates adenylate cyclase, increasing cyclic AMP and activating protein kinase A
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases** (insulin): binding activates receptor autophosphorylation and phosphorylation of intracellular proteins, initiating kinase and phosphatase cascades

### Insulin (fed state)

Secreted by pancreatic beta cells when blood glucose is high:

- Glucose uptake into insulin-responsive tissues
- Glycogen synthesis
- Glycolysis in liver
- Fatty-acid synthesis
- Amino-acid uptake and protein synthesis
- Inhibition of glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis

### Glucagon (fasting state)

Secreted by pancreatic alpha cells when blood glucose is low. Acts mainly on liver and adipose tissue:

- Stimulates liver glycogenolysis
- Stimulates gluconeogenesis
- Promotes adipose triacylglycerol breakdown
- Increases circulating glucose and fatty acids

Skeletal muscle does not respond to glucagon the same way as liver, but responds strongly to adrenaline during exercise.

### Adrenaline

Released from the adrenal medulla during stress and exercise. Promotes liver glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis, muscle glycogen breakdown and adipose lipolysis. Activates glycogen phosphorylase and inhibits glycogen synthase.

## Glycogen Metabolism

Highly branched glucose-storage polysaccharide of animals. Most abundant in **skeletal muscle** and **liver**.

- Main chains contain alpha glycosidic linkages
- Branches connected through alpha linkages approximately every ten glucose residues
- One reducing end, many non-reducing ends, allowing rapid simultaneous synthesis or breakdown

**Liver glycogen** supports blood-glucose maintenance for the whole body. **Muscle glycogen** is used locally because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

### Reciprocal regulation

| Modification | Glycogen synthase | Glycogen phosphorylase |
|---|---|---|
| Phosphorylation | Inactivated | Activated |
| Dephosphorylation | Activated | Inactivated |

Glucagon and adrenaline activate protein kinases and promote breakdown. Insulin promotes phosphatase activity and glycogen synthesis. AMP promotes breakdown; ATP and glucose phosphate favour storage.

### Glycogen storage diseases

Inherited defects in glycogen synthesis or degradation. **Liver** diseases may cause hepatomegaly and hypoglycaemia. **Muscle** diseases may cause cramps, weakness and exercise intolerance.

## Muscle Fibre Types

| Feature | Type I | Type II |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Red, high myoglobin | White, lower myoglobin |
| Mitochondria and capillaries | Many mitochondria, strong capillary supply | Fewer mitochondria, poorer supply |
| Contraction | Slow, lower force, fatigue-resistant | Fast, high force, fatigue-prone |
| Metabolism | Aerobic: glucose, fatty acids, ketones | Anaerobic emphasis: glucose and glycogen |

### Energy systems during exercise

1. Stored ATP supports the first few seconds
2. **Creatine phosphate** rapidly regenerates ATP for short intense exertion
3. **Anaerobic glycolysis** supports brief high-intensity activity, producing lactate
4. **Aerobic oxidation** supports longer exercise
5. **Fatty-acid oxidation** contributes increasingly during prolonged lower-intensity exercise

**Adenylate kinase** converts two ADP into ATP and AMP. The small AMP increase strongly activates glycolysis because AMP is a sensitive signal of energy stress.

## Clinical Integration

### Type 1 vs Type 2 diabetes

| Feature | Type 1 | Type 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Primary defect | Low or absent insulin secretion | Reduced tissue response to insulin, often with compensatory hyperinsulinaemia initially |
| Glucose uptake | Impaired because insulin is deficient | Impaired because tissues are insulin resistant |
| Metabolism | Increased lipolysis and ketogenesis | Insulin resistance, altered lipid metabolism, progressive glucose dysregulation |
| Useful tests | Fasting glucose, OGTT, insulin or C-peptide, ketones | Fasting glucose, OGTT, HbA1c, insulin, lipid profile |

### Metabolic syndrome pattern

Central obesity, elevated fasting glucose, high insulin and elevated triglycerides suggest insulin resistance and increased risk of type 2 diabetes.

### Phenylketonuria

Deficient **phenylalanine hydroxylase** prevents conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine. Phenylalanine and abnormal metabolites accumulate and can damage neurological development. Dietary restriction of phenylalanine is central to management.
