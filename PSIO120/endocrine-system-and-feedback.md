# Endocrine System, Feedback Loops and Axes

**Sources:** University of Newcastle HUBS1406, James Cook University, Neuroendocrine Cancer Australia (neuroendocrine.org.au), Studocu AU. All Australian.

---

## Negative Feedback Loops

Maintains stability by detecting a change and activating responses that **oppose** it.

1. **Stimulus** - a change occurs in the internal or external environment
2. **Receptor or sensor** - detects the change
3. **Control centre** - processes the information, determines a response
4. **Effector** - carries out the response
5. **Correction** - the response reduces the original change
6. **Return toward normal** - once corrected, the response is reduced or switched off

Example: increased thyroid hormone reduces stimulation of the hypothalamus and pituitary, preventing excessive production.

Positive feedback amplifies rather than opposes. Much rarer. Examples: oxytocin in labour, oestrogen surge before ovulation.

## The Neuroendocrine System

Manages communication between the nervous system and the endocrine system. Neuroendocrine cells act as both neurons (responding to neural inputs) and hormonal secretors (releasing hormones into blood).

**Two core functions:** regulating hormone secretion, and coordinating bodily functions.

### Key components

- **Hypothalamus** - control centre. Integrates nervous system signals, initiates endocrine responses. Secretes hormones that stimulate or suppress pituitary release
- **Pituitary gland** - the "master gland". Receives hypothalamic signals, produces hormones regulating growth, reproduction and stress response
- **Adrenal glands** - produce adrenaline and cortisol, critical for stress response

### Neuroendocrine vs endocrine

- **Endocrine:** glands secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream for effects on distant organs. Slower, better for long-term functions like metabolism, growth and development
- **Neuroendocrine:** neural inputs directly translated into hormonal outputs. Faster and more dynamic. Better at acute responses to environmental stimuli, stress, and circadian rhythms

## Key Hormones and Deficiency Signs

| Hormone | Produced by | Main effect | Effects of too little |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insulin | Pancreas | Reduces blood glucose by helping cells take up and store glucose | Raised blood glucose, impaired regulation |
| Glucagon | Pancreas | Raises blood glucose by releasing stored glucose | Difficulty maintaining glucose during fasting |
| Adrenaline | Adrenal glands | Supports fight-or-flight: increased heart activity and energy | Reduced ability to produce an effective stress response |
| Oxytocin | Hypothalamus, released from posterior pituitary | Promotes uterine contractions, supports milk ejection | Reduced uterine contraction or milk ejection |
| Antidiuretic hormone | Hypothalamus, released from posterior pituitary | Promotes water retention by the kidneys | Excessive water loss, large volumes of dilute urine |
| Growth hormone | Anterior pituitary | Supports growth, influences metabolism | Reduced growth, impaired tissue development |
| Thyroid hormone | Thyroid gland | Regulates metabolic activity | Tiredness, lethargy, weight gain (hypothyroidism) |
| Cortisol | Adrenal cortex | Supports stress response, regulates metabolism | Reduced stress response and metabolic regulation |

> Oxytocin and ADH are **produced** in the hypothalamus but **released** from the posterior pituitary.

## The Thyroid Hormone Axis

1. Hypothalamus releases **TRH** (thyrotropin-releasing hormone)
2. TRH stimulates the pituitary gland
3. Pituitary releases **TSH** (thyroid-stimulating hormone)
4. TSH stimulates the thyroid to produce **thyroid hormone** (T3/T4)
5. Rising thyroid hormone provides **negative feedback**, reducing TRH and TSH

**Summary:** Hypothalamus -> TRH -> pituitary -> TSH -> thyroid -> thyroid hormone

### Clinical interpretation

Patient reports tiredness, lethargy and weight gain. Blood tests show **high TSH (12.4)** and **low thyroid hormone (7.8)**.

**Diagnosis: primary hypothyroidism** - the problem is the thyroid gland itself.

**Reasoning:** the thyroid is not producing enough hormone. Because thyroid hormone is low, negative feedback is reduced. The pituitary therefore releases MORE TSH attempting to stimulate the failing thyroid. High TSH with low thyroid hormone locates the problem in the gland rather than the pituitary or hypothalamus.

> If the pituitary were the problem, TSH would be LOW as well as T4.

## Water Balance and Blood Pressure Hormones

### ADH pathway

1. Hypothalamus detects increased blood **osmolarity**
2. ADH released from the posterior pituitary
3. Collecting ducts of the nephron produce **aquaporins**
4. Increased water reabsorption
5. Restored normal osmolarity provides negative feedback, reducing ADH release

| Hormone | Type | Source | Release control | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ADH | Water-soluble peptide | Hypothalamus, posterior pituitary | Hypothalamus detects osmolarity change | Collecting ducts produce aquaporins, increasing water reabsorption |

### Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)

1. Kidney releases **renin**
2. Renin converts angiotensinogen into **angiotensin I**
3. Angiotensin I converted to **angiotensin II**
4. Angiotensin II stimulates the adrenal cortex to release **aldosterone**
5. Aldosterone promotes **sodium and water retention**, supporting fluid balance and blood pressure

| Hormone | Type | Source | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Renin | Protein enzyme | Kidney | Converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I |
| Angiotensin I | Peptide | From angiotensinogen | Intermediate, converted to angiotensin II |
| Angiotensin II | Peptide | From angiotensin I | Stimulates aldosterone, regulates BP and fluid |
| Aldosterone | Steroid | Adrenal cortex | Sodium and water retention |

> This is the pathway ACE inhibitors and ARBs interrupt to lower blood pressure. Aldosterone conserves sodium and excretes potassium.

## Stress and Metabolic Hormones

| Hormone | Type | Source | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adrenaline | Water-soluble catecholamine | Adrenal medulla | Sympathetic stress response | Rapid stress-response changes |
| Norepinephrine | Water-soluble catecholamine | Adrenal medulla and sympathetic nerve endings | Sympathetic activation | Supports rapid stress-response effects |
| ACTH | Protein | Anterior pituitary | Hypothalamic control | Stimulates adrenal cortex to release cortisol |
| Cortisol | Steroid | Adrenal cortex | Response to ACTH | Longer-term stress response |
| Glucagon | Water-soluble protein | Pancreatic alpha cells | Low blood glucose | Raises blood glucose |
| Insulin | Water-soluble protein | Pancreatic beta cells | High blood glucose | Lowers blood glucose via uptake and storage |

### The stress response pathway

1. Hypothalamus detects the stressor (direct neural input or hormonal signals)
2. Releases **CRH** (corticotropin-releasing hormone) into the bloodstream, targeting the pituitary
3. CRH stimulates the pituitary to secrete **ACTH**
4. ACTH travels through the bloodstream to the adrenal glands
5. Adrenals release **cortisol** and **adrenaline**
6. Rising cortisol signals the hypothalamus and pituitary to reduce CRH and ACTH (**negative feedback**)

**Adrenaline:** increases heart rate, elevates blood pressure, boosts energy supplies.

**Cortisol:** increases blood glucose, enhances the brain's use of glucose, curbs non-essential functions.

**Psychological effects:** sharpen focus and increase alertness. Chronic activation leads to anxiety, depression or sleep disturbance.

## Reproductive Hormones

### Male pathway

Hypothalamus -> **GnRH** -> Anterior pituitary -> **FSH** and **LH**

| Hormone | Source | Action | Feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| GnRH | Hypothalamus | Controls FSH and LH release | Initiates the pathway |
| FSH | Anterior pituitary | Supports sperm production, stimulates Sertoli cells | Inhibited by inhibin and testosterone |
| LH | Anterior pituitary | Stimulates interstitial (Leydig) cells to produce testosterone | Inhibited by testosterone |
| Testosterone | Interstitial cells of testes | Sperm production, secondary male characteristics | Negative feedback inhibits FSH and LH |
| Inhibin | Sertoli cells | Regulates sperm production | Inhibits FSH release |
| Androgen-binding protein | Sertoli cells | Concentrates testosterone in seminiferous tubules | Stimulated by FSH |

### Female pathway

Hypothalamus -> **GnRH** -> Anterior pituitary -> **FSH** and **LH** -> Ovaries

| Hormone | Source | Action |
|---|---|---|
| FSH | Anterior pituitary | Stimulates ovarian follicle development |
| LH | Anterior pituitary | Regulates ovarian hormone production and reproductive events |
| Oestrogen | Ovaries | Supports female reproductive development and function |
| Progesterone | Ovaries | Supports reproductive function after ovulation |
| hCG | Early pregnancy tissue | Supports the early pregnancy hormonal pathway |

## Neuron Structure

- **Dendrites** - receive signals from other cells
- **Cell body** - contains the nucleus, maintains the cell
- **Axon** - carries nerve impulses away from the cell body
- **Myelin sheath** - insulates the axon, helps impulses travel efficiently
- **Axon terminals** - release chemical signals to communicate with another cell

## Non-Australian reference only (NOT for the app)

From Hiller-Sturmhofel and Bartke, *The Endocrine System: An Overview* (US source). Structure and mnemonics only.

### Three hormone classes

| Class | Structure | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steroids | Cholesterol-like | Enter cells, bind cytoplasmic or nuclear receptors, regulate gene activity | Cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone |
| Amino acid derivatives | Modified amino acids | Enter cells, interact with DNA-bound receptors | Thyroid hormone, adrenaline |
| Polypeptides and proteins | Amino acid chains | Cannot enter cells. Bind surface receptors, trigger intracellular cascades | Insulin, glucagon, GH, ACTH, ADH, oxytocin |

### The three major axes

**HPA axis (stress):** Hypothalamus (CRH) -> Pituitary (ACTH) -> Adrenals (cortisol). Negative feedback: cortisol represses CRH and ACTH. Disorders: Addison's disease (low cortisol, high ACTH), Cushing's syndrome (high cortisol, low ACTH).

**HPG axis (reproductive):** Hypothalamus (GnRH) -> Pituitary (FSH + LH) -> Gonads (sex hormones). Negative feedback normally. Exception: positive feedback from oestradiol just before ovulation drives the LH surge.

**HPT axis (metabolic):** Hypothalamus (TRH) -> Pituitary (TSH) -> Thyroid (T3/T4). Negative feedback from T3 and T4 on both hypothalamus and pituitary.

### Short-loop feedback

In some instances pituitary hormones act directly back on the hypothalamus rather than waiting for the target gland hormone.
