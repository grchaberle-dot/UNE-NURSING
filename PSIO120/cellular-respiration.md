# Cellular Respiration

**Sources:** Monash University Student Academic Success (monash.edu), VCE Biology Cell Respiration notes, WACE Year 11, University of Sydney. All Australian.

## Overview

Cellular respiration is the process cells use to make useable energy as ATP. Two forms:

- **Aerobic** requires oxygen, highest ATP yield
- **Anaerobic** does not require oxygen, lower yield but faster ATP production

Aerobic respiration requires mitochondria and occurs over three stages: glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain.

**Overall equation:** Glucose + Oxygen -> Carbon Dioxide + Water + **30/32 ATP**

---

## The Mitochondria

Double membrane structure:

| Component | Structure and function |
|---|---|
| Outer membrane | Smooth and permeable. Contains transport proteins |
| Inner membrane | Folded into **cristae**. Holds ETC and ATP synthase |
| Cristae | Folds raise SA:Vol ratio, optimising electron transport |
| Intermembrane space | Small space to rapidly accumulate protons |
| Matrix | Appropriate enzymes and pH for the Krebs cycle |

**Endosymbiosis evidence:** circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membrane. Believed to have evolved when an aerobic bacterium was engulfed by another prokaryotic cell.

---

## Stage 1: Glycolysis

- Location: **cytoplasm**
- Does NOT require oxygen
- Glucose (6-carbon) splits into 2 pyruvate (3-carbon)
- Initial stages are endergonic and consume 2 ATP. Gross gain 4 ATP, **net gain 2 ATP**

| Inputs | Outputs |
|---|---|
| Glucose | Pyruvate |
| ADP + Pi | 2 ATP (net) |
| NAD+ | NADH |

---

## Stage 2: Krebs Cycle

- Location: **mitochondrial matrix**
- Processes pyruvate via an intermediate step converting pyruvate to acetyl CoA
- Does NOT use oxygen
- Releases CO2 as waste
- **Yield: 2 ATP** per glucose, plus loaded electron carriers for Stage 3

| Inputs | Outputs |
|---|---|
| Pyruvate | Carbon dioxide |
| ADP + Pi | 2 ATP |
| NAD+ | NADH |
| FAD | FADH2 |

---

## Stage 3: Electron Transport Chain

- Location: **inner mitochondrial membrane**
- Transfers electrons from NADH and FADH2 to oxygen, forming water
- Hydrogen ions move through protein complexes creating a **proton gradient**
- **ATP synthase** uses that gradient to synthesise ATP from ADP + Pi
- **Net yield: 26 or 28 ATP**

| Inputs | Outputs |
|---|---|
| O2 | H2O |
| ADP + Pi | 26/28 ATP |
| NADH | NAD+ |
| FADH2 | FAD |

---

## Full ATP Yield Breakdown per Glucose

| Source | NADH | FADH2 | Oxidative ATP | Substrate-level ATP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | 2 | 0 | 3 | 2 (net) |
| Pyruvate to acetyl-CoA | 2 | 0 | 5 | 0 |
| Krebs cycle | 6 | 2 | 18 | 2 |
| **TOTAL** | **10** | **2** | **26** | **4** |

**Total net ATP per glucose (aerobic): ~30 ATP**

## Energy Conversions

Two coenzyme types carry energy:

- **ATP** is the immediately available energy source. Energy released when hydrolysed to ADP
- **Hydrogen carriers** (NADH, FADH2) are the transitional energy source, carrying high energy electrons and protons

**Two production methods:**

- **Substrate-level phosphorylation:** ATP made directly from organic molecules (glycolysis, Krebs)
- **Oxidative phosphorylation:** ATP synthesised indirectly using hydrogen carriers. Requires O2 (ETC)

> Analogy: glucose is the piggy bank (stored energy), hydrogen carriers are the wallet (transitional), ATP is the cash (usable).

## Anaerobic Respiration

Produces ATP without oxygen. Also called anaerobic fermentation. Occurs **entirely in the cytosol**. Lower ATP yield per glucose but faster production speed.

### Lactic Acid Fermentation (animals)

Human muscle cells during intense exercise or explosive bursts when oxygen cannot be delivered fast enough.

**Equation:** Glucose -> Lactic Acid

Examples: 100m sprinters, weightlifters, cheetah chasing prey.

**REVERSIBLE.** Lactic acid converts back to pyruvate once exercise ends, then gets digested aerobically.

| Inputs | Outputs |
|---|---|
| Glucose | Lactic acid |
| ADP + Pi | 2 ATP |
| NAD+ | NAD+ |

### Alcoholic Fermentation (yeast and plants)

**Equation:** Glucose -> Ethanol + 2CO2

Important in brewing, winemaking, baking. **IRREVERSIBLE.**

| Inputs | Outputs |
|---|---|
| Glucose | Ethanol |
| ADP + Pi | Carbon dioxide |
| NAD+ | 2 ATP, NAD+ |

### Why fermentation happens

In the absence of oxygen, hydrogen carriers must be unloaded (NADH back to NAD+) so glycolysis can continue. Fermentation produces no extra ATP itself.

## Aerobic vs Anaerobic

| Feature | Aerobic | Anaerobic |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose breakdown | Complete | Partial |
| Oxygen supply | Plentiful | Little or none |
| Organisms | Plants, animals, bacteria, fungi | Yeasts, some bacteria, animal muscle at times |
| Steps | Glycolysis, Krebs, ETC | Glycolysis only |
| Inputs | Glucose + Oxygen | Glucose |
| Outputs | CO2 + Water | Lactic acid OR Ethanol + CO2 |
| ATP per glucose | 30 or 32 | 2 |
| Location | Cytoplasm + Mitochondria | Cytoplasm only |

## Factors Affecting the Rate

### Temperature (bell curve)

Enzymes in glycolysis, Krebs and the ETC work best at an optimal range.

- **Low temperatures:** enzymatic activity slows, reducing the rate
- **High temperatures:** enzymes **denature**, impairing function and stopping respiration

### Glucose concentration (plateau)

Glucose is the primary fuel and the starting point for glycolysis. Rate rises then plateaus once the environment is saturated and another factor becomes limiting. If glucose is low, cells may use fatty acids or amino acids, but these yield less ATP or are less efficient.

Measured by monitoring CO2 output relative to glucose concentration.

### Oxygen concentration (plateau)

Oxygen is needed to keep the ETC accepting de-energised electrons. If limited, cells shift to anaerobic respiration. Low oxygen causes buildup of lactate (animals) or ethanol and CO2 (yeast).

### Exercise intensity

Strenuous exertion means ATP requirements exceed oxygen intake, so anaerobic respiration is used. Lactic acid accumulates in muscle causing fatigue, making high-intensity exercise unsustainable. Aerobic respiration resumes as activity diminishes.

> Temperature gives a bell curve (denaturation). Glucose and oxygen give a plateau (saturation).

## Biotechnological Applications

**Cellular respiration pathways** exploited to enhance fermentation technologies, enabling production of biofuels (ethanol) and bioplastics.

**Photosynthesis pathway** is the foundation for artificial photosynthesis and genetic modification of crops (CRISPR-Cas9) to increase photosynthetic efficiency.

### Biofuels

Energy from anaerobic fermentation of **biomass** (organic material from plants or animals).

**Advantages:** renewable, lower carbon footprint (biomass produced via photosynthesis which uses CO2).

**Biomass sources:**
- Agricultural feedstocks (edible crops): needs lots of arable land, drives up food prices
- Non-edible plant components and municipal wastes: higher production costs
- Algae: photosynthesises cheaply, low land use (photobioreactor)

**Bioethanol drawbacks:** lower energy output than fossil fuels, harder to vaporise (difficult in cold), more likely to corrode materials.

These advances address food security and climate change.

## Non-Australian reference only (NOT for the app)

From Pearson General Biology study notes (US source). Structure and mnemonics only.

**Redox reactions:** Oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain. Mnemonic **OIL RIG**. In respiration, glucose is oxidised to CO2 and oxygen is reduced to water.

**Proton leak and thermogenesis:** protons diffuse back into the matrix through channels other than ATP synthase (uncoupling proteins). Exergonic, releases heat. Important in brown adipose tissue. Decreases ATP efficiency.

**Reactive oxygen species:** the ETC can produce superoxide (O2-), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radicals. Can damage lipids, proteins and DNA. Linked to aging and cancer.
